A festival is a city that exists for three days. It has a border with a gate, a currency that lives in your wristband, a water supply, a sewer system that is mostly plastic, a hospital, a police presence, a transit network and a skyline made of scaffolding. Then it is taken apart, and the field goes back to being a field.

It is also a business, and a bigger one than it looks from the front of the crowd. Live Nation alone says it operated "131 festivals globally in 2025."1 The license at Glastonbury allowed up to 210,000 people on site in 2025, counting fans, staff and performers.2 At the other end of the market, the UK's Association of Independent Festivals counted a record 78 festivals lost in 2024 alone.3

This guide explains how all of it works: why the headliner's name is so big on the poster, why tickets go up in tiers, why you cannot slide the band off your wrist, how many toilets a crowd needs, what the safety rules learned the hard way, who keeps the money left on your cashless balance, and when the whole thing happens around the world. It opens with a finder for the big festivals of the world, then starts the history in Newport, Rhode Island, in 1954.

Every factual claim is footnoted to the festival, filing, report or reporting it came from, and every price, fee and crowd figure carries its year. Sentences without a footnote are our own explanation. Where a fact could not be pinned down, it is listed in the open questions at the end rather than guessed at. This guide is independent and not affiliated with any festival or promoter; the people who run them, work them and play them are welcome at the corrections desk.

    02A short history of the music festival

    The short version: The outdoor festival as most people know it - a long bill of acts, more than one day, a resort town or a field, a ticket sold by the day - is usually traced to Newport, Rhode Island, in 1954, though Los Angeles had an annual jazz event before that.4 The late 1960s showed both the pull of the idea and the cost of losing control of it: Woodstock drew an estimated 460,000 to 500,000 people in 1969 against a plan for about 50,000.5 Britain and Denmark then made the camping festival an annual event, and the form has since toured, settled into destination sites, boomed on dance music, stopped for the pandemic and, since 2023, been squeezed hard: one UK trade body counted a record 78 festivals lost in 2024.3

    Where did the music festival start?

    The modern festival template started in Newport, Rhode Island, in 1954, paid for by a society couple. Elaine and Louis Lorillard financed a jazz weekend at the Newport Casino in 1954 and hired George Wein to organize it; it was billed as the "First Annual American Jazz Festival," and about 13,000 people came over two days (Wikipedia figure).4 Opening night was July 17, 1954, with tickets at "$3 to $5" (1954, reported) and a bill of Billie Holiday, Dizzy Gillespie, Gerry Mulligan, Oscar Peterson and the Modern Jazz Quartet; the next night brought Ella Fitzgerald and a Count Basie tribute.6

    The "first festival" title is disputed. Wikipedia notes that "earlier large-scale annual jazz festivals had already been established," pointing to the Cavalcade of Jazz at Wrigley Field in Los Angeles, which started in 1945.4 What Newport did set was the shape: a fixed summer date, a destination town, a bill long enough to fill a weekend, and a name you could sell again next year.

    Newport also produced an early lesson about the gate. In 1960, sold-out crowds who could not get in rioted; "Some 200 people were arrested, a town record," the National Guard was called and the last two days were canceled. Charles Mingus and Max Roach ran a rival festival nearby the same weekend.4

    What happened when Dylan went electric at Newport?

    On July 25, 1965, Bob Dylan played his first "plugged-in" professional live set at Newport Folk, backed by members of the Paul Butterfield Blues Band, and gave "Like a Rolling Stone" its first public performance.7 The booing is the famous part, and the accounts do not agree. Wikipedia says it came from "a small number of folk 'purists'" and that eyewitness reaction was "generally quite enthusiastic."7

    Newport Folk had started in 1959 at Freebody Park, founded by George Wein, Albert Grossman, Pete Seeger, Theodore Bikel and Oscar Brand.7 It was far from continuous. It did not run in 1961-1962 or from 1970 to 1984, and came back in 1985 at Fort Adams.7

    In New England: what Newport looks like now

    As of 2026, both Newport festivals run at Fort Adams State Park under the same non-profit owner. The jazz festival moved to New York City in 1972, came back to Newport at Fort Adams in 1981, and since 2011 has been run by the Newport Festivals Foundation, which also runs the folk festival.4 That is a different model from the promoter-owned majors: the surplus goes to a foundation rather than shareholders.

    Demand is not the problem. Newport Folk sold out all three days "months in advance" in 2014 and every year after that through 2023, according to Wikipedia.7

    Fort Adams is small by big-festival standards: Rhode Island's governor's office described capacity crowds there as 10,000 in 2023.8 Boston Calling, the region's other big name, debuted at Boston's City Hall Plaza in May 2013 with early crowds of about 20,000-22,000 (Wikipedia figure);9 the New England section tells both stories in full.

    What made Monterey Pop different?

    Monterey Pop ran like a benefit: the artists played for free and revenue went to charity, with one exception on the festival's side - Ravi Shankar, who was paid $3,000 (1967).10 The Monterey International Pop Festival (June 16-18, 1967, Monterey County Fairgrounds) was put together in seven weeks by John Phillips, Lou Adler, Alan Pariser and Derek Taylor.10

    It is remembered as a launch pad. Monterey gave the Jimi Hendrix Experience and the Who their first major US appearances and Janis Joplin her first large-scale show.10 The crowd numbers are loose: estimates run from 25,000 to 90,000, against an arena approved for 7,000 that held about 8,500 on Saturday night, with tickets from $3 to $6.50 (1967, Wikipedia figures).10

    How many people went to Woodstock, and did it lose money?

    An estimated 460,000 to 500,000 people went to Woodstock, against a plan for about 50,000, and yes, it left its backers deep in debt. It ran August 15-18, 1969 - it was scheduled to end on the 17th - on Max Yasgur's dairy farm in Bethel, New York, with 32 acts. The promoters, trading as Woodstock Ventures, were Michael Lang, Artie Kornfeld, Joel Rosenman and John P. Roberts.5

    Tickets were $18 in advance and $24 at the gate (1969), and about 186,000 advance tickets were sold. It became a "free concert" because the fences and ticket booths were not finished by opening day.5 Three deaths were recorded.5

    As a ticketed event it was a disaster for the people who paid for it. Roberts and Rosenman were left near ruin and faced about 80 lawsuits; earnings from the film and the soundtrack album eventually paid off $1.4 million in debt and the settlements.5 That is an early version of a lesson the business still uses: the rights to film and record a festival can be worth as much as the gate.

    What happened at Altamont?

    Four months after Woodstock, a free concert at Altamont Speedway near Livermore, California, ended with four deaths. UPI's report two days later put the crowd at "upwards of 300,000" and recorded the deaths - Meredith Hunter, 18, stabbed near the stage during the Rolling Stones' set; one drowning; and two men struck by a car - as well as two births. Hells Angels attacked people near the stage.11

    The lesson the business took is about planning, and the safety section follows it to the present: a free show put together at speed, in front of a crowd that size, with security near the stage that made things worse. The events that lasted were the ones that planned for the crowd they would actually get.

    How did Britain end up with the camping festival?

    The American 1960s events were mostly one-offs; the British ones came back every year, and the camping festival grew out of that habit.

    Isle of Wight 1970 (August 26-30, Afton Down) was the biggest crowd of the era: Guinness World Records estimated 600,000 to 700,000 people. When it became clear there would be no profit, the organizers declared it a free festival.12 The response was legal. The Isle of Wight County Council Act 1971 added sections "designed to control any further large overnight gatherings," and the island had no event of its kind for 32 years.12

    Glastonbury started the same year, much smaller. Michael Eavis held the first one in September 1970, the day after Jimi Hendrix died: £1 a ticket, free milk from the farm, 1,500 people, and a bill with Marc Bolan, Al Stewart and Quintessence (official history).13 It was a one-day "Pop, Folk & Blues" event.14 In 1971 it was renamed Glastonbury Fair and the first Pyramid Stage went up.15

    Reading has the same jazz-to-rock arc as Newport. It began as the National Jazz Festival in August 1961 at Richmond Athletic Ground, became the National Jazz and Blues Festival in 1965, moved through Windsor, Kempton Park (1968) and Plumpton (1969), and settled in Reading in 1971. A second site at Leeds was added in 1999; the local council canceled the 1984 and 1985 events.16

    Why does Glastonbury take a year off?

    Glastonbury takes planned fallow years - years with no festival - to "give the land, locals, and organisers a break." The first was 1988. The 1991 fallow year followed clashes between travelers and security in 1990, and further fallow years came in 1996, 2001, 2006, 2012 and 2018. In 2020 (canceled March 18) and 2021 (canceled January 21) Covid forced what the fan archive calls an "enforced fallow year."15

    2026 is a fallow year. The next festival is set for June 23-27, 2027, on a 900-acre (about 364-hectare) dairy farm that hosts about 200,000 people (reported).17 For scale against 1970: in 2003 a ticket was £150, 150,000 tickets were on offer and "more than £1m" was raised for charity (2003, reported by the Daily Mirror).14

    Elsewhere in the world: Roskilde and the non-profit model

    Denmark's Roskilde started on August 28-29, 1971 as the "Sound Festival," with 10,000 to 13,000 people paying 30 Danish kroner (1971). Its founders were Mogens Sandfær, Jesper Switzer Møller and promoter Carl Fischer.18 It is run by the Roskilde Foundation as a non-profit that "still relies heavily on thousands of volunteers," with attendance of more than 130,000 in 2024 and a ticket of 2,600 kroner in 2026 (Wikipedia figures).18

    Roskilde is also the festival the safety world studies. On June 30, 2000, a crowd crush at the Orange Stage during Pearl Jam's set killed nine people and injured 26.18

    What was the touring festival?

    Lollapalooza turned the festival into a package tour: one bill, moving city to city. It opened on July 18, 1991, at Compton Terrace in Arizona, credited to Perry Farrell working with booking agents including Marc Geiger, and began as a farewell tour for Jane's Addiction.19 Sources differ on whether to call that first city Chandler or Tempe; Compton Terrace sits between the two.

    The 1991 tour ran 20 dates through July and August with Jane's Addiction, Siouxsie and the Banshees, Nine Inch Nails, Living Colour, Butthole Surfers, Rollins Band, Fishbone, Ice-T with Body Count and Violent Femmes. It toured until 1997 and came back in 2005 as a yearly event in Chicago.20 In Grant Park it "typically welcomes 100,000 daily attendees" (reported, 2021).21

    What is a destination festival?

    Between about 1997 and 2005 a new kind of festival appeared: one fixed site, often with camping, sold as a trip as much as a show. Coachella, first held in 1999, belongs in this group; its story is told in full in the sister guide to Coachella.

    Fuji Rock had the roughest start. The 1997 edition at Fuji Tenjinyama Ski Resort was hit by a typhoon on day one, day two was canceled and there were cases of hypothermia. After a year in Tokyo, it has run for three days at Naeba, Niigata, since 1999, organized by Smash with the stated aim of being "The cleanest festival in the world." It drew a record 140,000 over three days in 2012 and 122,000 in 2025 (Wikipedia figures).22

    Primavera Sound opened on April 28, 2001 at Poble Espanyol in Barcelona with 7,700 people, moved in 2005 to the Parc del Fòrum (a site "seven times larger"), and added Porto from 2012.23 Bonnaroo's first edition (June 21-23, 2002) sold "More than 70,000 tickets" in advance.24 It was founded by Superfly and AC Entertainment (Ashley Capps) with Coran Capshaw and Chip Hooper on a 700-acre (about 283-hectare) farm in Manchester, Tennessee.25

    Tomorrowland began in August 2005 at De Schorre in Boom, Belgium, with about 10,000 people. It reached 180,000 in 2010 and 400,000 across two weekends from 2017, with a capacity of 200,000 per weekend (Wikipedia figures).26 It sold the trip outright: its Global Journey packages with Brussels Airlines carried 2,000 passengers from 17 cities in 2012 and 8,000 from 67 cities in 2013.26

    Why is EDC in Las Vegas?

    EDC moved to Las Vegas in 2011 after Los Angeles capped its crowd following a teenager's death after the 2010 event. Insomniac was founded in October 1993 and held the first Electric Daisy Carnival in 1997 at the Shrine Expo Hall in Los Angeles.27 By 2010 EDC drew "more than 160,000" people to the Los Angeles Coliseum over two days, according to the Las Vegas Sun.28

    That year a 15-year-old, Sasha Rodriguez, died of an ecstasy overdose after attending. The Coliseum Commission then capped capacity at 75,000 per day, and EDC left Los Angeles (reported, February 2011).28 It moved to Las Vegas Motor Speedway in 2011, and Insomniac reports 465,000 attendees over three days in 2019 (official).27

    What happened during the EDM boom?

    Electronic dance music drove the biggest growth and the biggest deal-making of the 2010s. Robert F. X. Sillerman revived SFX Entertainment, a name he had first used in 1996, in June 2012 and bought fast: Beatport in February 2013, a 75% stake in ID&T (Tomorrowland's organizer) announced the following month, Made Event (Electric Zoo) and i-Motion ($21 million) in November 2013, and 75% of Paylogic that December. SFX began trading on NASDAQ on October 9, 2013.29 It filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on February 1, 2016, and emerged that December as the private company LiveStyle after cutting $400 million of debt; ID&T went to Superstruct in 2021, in a deal that excluded Tomorrowland.29

    Live Nation took a different route. It confirmed a partnership with Insomniac in June 2013 without disclosing terms; the Wall Street Journal had reported the month before that Live Nation was about to pay about $50 million for a stake. Insomniac founder Pasquale Rotella said in an online statement that "the Insomniac team, led by myself, will maintain complete creative control of all events" and that "we are still a stand-alone company."303132

    What happened to festivals during Covid?

    Almost every major festival was canceled in 2020. Lollapalooza called off its 2020 edition on June 9 and replaced it with a livestream from July 30 to August 2.36 Coachella first moved from April to October and then lost the October dates when Riverside County's health officer canceled them; about 40% of ticket buyers had asked for refunds (reported, June 2020).37 Glastonbury canceled 2020 on March 18 and 2021 on January 21,15 and Roskilde canceled both years.18

    US festivals came back in the second half of 2021: Lollapalooza returned July 29 to August 1, 2021 "at full capacity," with proof of vaccination or a negative test required.21 Glastonbury and Roskilde waited until 2022. Primavera folded most of its canceled 2020 and 2021 bookings into a two-weekend 2022 edition that drew 460,500 (Wikipedia figure).23 Not everyone came back: the UK's Association of Independent Festivals counts 96 festivals lost to Covid.38

    No official global tally of 2020 cancellations was found. One early private count: as of April 1, 2020, the music analytics company Viberate counted "over 550 music festivals" canceled or postponed because of Covid-19, which it estimated at around 11% of all music festivals (estimate, partial year).39 Pollstar put the live music industry's lost revenue for 2020 at more than $30 billion (estimate, as summarized by Wikipedia).40

    Why are so many festivals being canceled?

    The reasons given vary: tax and low ticket sales in the UK, owner budget cuts and a crowded market in the US, and bad weather. The firmest counts are British. They come from the Association of Independent Festivals (AIF), a trade and lobby group for independent events, so they reflect its members' view.

    AIF counted 36 festivals canceled, postponed or closed in 2023 and 72 in 2024 as of late November that year, with 204 "disappeared" since 2019.38 By June 2025 it had revised 2024 to a final 78, a record, and counted 249 lost since 2019 and 39 more lost by June 10, 2025.3 The full 2025 total was 43; in 2026, WOMAD Glasgow became the 20th of the year, citing low ticket sales (reported).41

    AIF's CEO, John Rostron, called it "a devastating period for the UK's festival organisers." The group asked for a "temporary lowering" of VAT on festival tickets from 20% to 5%, which it says "would have saved most of the events that have closed this year" (November 2024).38 In 2025 it proposed a festival tax relief for events under 30,000 capacity, modeled on the reliefs for theaters and orchestras. Secret Garden Party said 2024 would be its last.3

    The US has no official count. Linking to the listings site Music Festival Wizard for its 2025 figure, Digital Music News reported in July 2025 that more than 90 festivals had been canceled "in 2025 alone" and over 170 in 2024 - counts the article frames globally rather than as US-only - and named Pitchfork Music Festival, Atlanta's Music Midtown and Connecticut's Soundside among the US losses.42 In September 2026 Web Smith of the newsletter 2PM counted roughly 30 US cancellations in 2025 and about 28 in 2026 through early September on the most-cited aggregator's list (estimates), and wrote that "the United States has no official tracker."43

    Pitchfork Music Festival ended its Chicago run after 19 years (announced November 11, 2024); supporters blamed budget cuts at owner Condé Nast, while others pointed to "a struggling market for music festivals in general."44 Primavera's Los Angeles edition (September 2022) did not continue, which Wikipedia attributes to "an oversaturated American market" and scheduling.23

    Weather adds to the strain. Bonnaroo 2025 was canceled after opening night because of severe weather, and Wikipedia lists its 2026 attendance at about 45,000, against 70,000 in 2024.25 Tomorrowland's 2025 main stage burned on July 16, two days before opening, and a smaller replacement was built by July 18.26

      03Who owns the festivals

      The short version: As of 2026, two US companies, Live Nation and AEG, sit behind most of the biggest American festivals, while KKR-owned Superstruct and the German ticketing group CTS Eventim hold the largest portfolios in Europe. Most of the big deals follow one pattern: a large promoter buys a controlling stake and the founder stays on to run the show. Plenty of festivals are still independent, Glastonbury the most famous among them. Who owns a festival rarely shows on the poster, but it can shape the ticket site, the sponsors and where else your favorite act is allowed to play that summer.

      Who are the big festival owners in 2026?

      The largest owner by count is Live Nation, which says in its annual report that it operated "131 festivals globally in 2025" and calls itself "one of the largest music festival producers in the world."1 AEG, its main US rival, says it runs 25 music festivals and promotes more than 12,000 shows.45 In Europe, Superstruct Entertainment runs "over 80 music festivals" in 10 countries across Europe and Australia, and has been owned by the investment firm KKR since a deal announced in June 2024.46 CTS Eventim's festival arm, EVENTIM LIVE, says it has "more than 50 formats" across more than 45 promoters.47

      How did Live Nation end up with Lollapalooza?

      Live Nation got Lollapalooza by buying into its promoter. Lollapalooza in Chicago is run by C3 Presents, which also runs Austin City Limits. In December 2014, Live Nation bought a reported 51% of C3 for a reported $125 million, which brought Lollapalooza's editions in Chicago, Berlin, Buenos Aires, Santiago and São Paulo under its roof.48

      The dance side moved first. In June 2013, Live Nation and Insomniac, the company behind Electric Daisy Carnival Las Vegas, announced a partnership with no terms disclosed. The Wall Street Journal had reported the month before that Live Nation was about to pay about $50 million for a stake, and a $100 million figure also circulated. Insomniac founder Pasquale Rotella said in an online statement that "the Insomniac team, led by myself, will maintain complete creative control of all events" and that "we are still a stand-alone company."303132

      Both deals fit the pattern this section keeps coming back to: the buyer gets the brand, the cash flow and a seat at the table, and the people who built the festival keep booking it.

      Who owns Coachella?

      Coachella is produced by Goldenvoice, part of AEG Presents, which also lists Stagecoach and Panorama among its festivals.49 AEG has bought into other events too: in July 2018 it bought out Red Frog Events' remaining shares of Firefly, in Dover, Delaware, taking full ownership.49 For how Coachella itself works on the ground, the sister guide to Coachella has the long version.

      AEG and Live Nation differ in one structural way that matters to fans. Live Nation's annual report lists a ticketing segment that brought in $3.1 billion in 2025, alongside concerts and sponsorship.1 That means a Live Nation festival can be booked, promoted, sponsored and ticketed inside one company.

      What did KKR buy when it bought Superstruct?

      Superstruct is the youngest of the big owners. James Barton and Roderik Schlösser founded it in 2017, and by 2024 it ran more than 80 festivals in 10 countries, from Wacken Open Air to Sziget to Sónar.46 The seller, the private equity firm Providence Equity Partners, announced the sale to KKR on June 21, 2024. The price was not disclosed, and Providence kept an option to invest €250 million in the business.46 Digital Music News, linking to the Financial Times, called it a "reported $1.4 billion (€1.3 billion) deal."51

      Private equity firms such as KKR and Providence buy companies to grow them and, eventually, sell them on. Superstruct went from one private equity owner to another, which is a normal life cycle for a portfolio company and a reason to watch for further changes.

      The portfolio has kept growing under KKR: in January 2025 Superstruct bought Boiler Room, the UK electronic music streaming platform, from the ticketing company DICE for an undisclosed sum.52 Wikipedia reports that Alex Mahon was named chief executive in April 2025.53

      Where does CTS Eventim fit?

      CTS Eventim is best known for selling tickets, and its festivals sit with the promoters it owns. Its 2024 revenue was €2.809 billion, up 19.1%, with €1.971 billion from live entertainment, and that year it bought Vivendi's ticketing and festival business, including See Tickets, for €300 million.54 In 2026 its EVENTIM LIVE arm reported "more than one million total attendees" across its festivals by July 10, naming Rock am Ring, Rock im Park, Hurricane, Southside and Nova Rock.47

      Which festivals are still independent?

      Glastonbury is the best-known holdout. Michael Eavis founded it in 1970, and in October 2024 he transferred his shares to his daughter Emily Eavis. He remains a director.5055 Independence does not mean no partners: the festival's partners include the BBC, the Co-op and Vodafone, and Neil Young said on his blog that the BBC arrangement left Glastonbury "now under corporate control."55 Its ownership, per Companies House filings reported by DJ Mag, stays with the family.50

      Most festivals in the world are small, local and owned by whoever started them, and they rarely make headlines about ownership.

      In New England

      The region's best-known festivals sit on opposite sides of the ownership pattern. Boston Calling, founded in 2013 by Brian Appel and Mike Snow, both formerly of the radio station WFNX,9 sold a controlling interest to The Madison Square Garden Company on July 6, 2016, with Appel, its CEO, staying on to help run the company.56 The Boston Globe reported in June 2025 that the company and MSG split in 2022 and that by 2023 it was a Live Nation subsidiary, with C3 Presents named as producer.57

      Newport Folk and Newport Jazz, in Rhode Island, have been run since 2011 by the Newport Festivals Foundation, which George Wein set up, according to Wikipedia.4 The foundation is a 501(c)(3) nonprofit focused on music education and artist relief, so there are no shareholders to pay.58 The New England section has the full story of both.

      Elsewhere in the world

      Ownership looks different by region. In the US, two companies own venues, festivals and, in Live Nation's case, a ticketing business.1 Continental Europe has more private equity (Superstruct) and ticketing-led groups (CTS Eventim).4654 The UK still has large family-run festivals such as Glastonbury alongside festivals in corporate portfolios.46 In Latin America, the most visible international brand is Lollapalooza, with editions in Buenos Aires, Santiago and São Paulo under Live Nation's C3.48

      What does ownership change for a festivalgoer?

      Often less than people expect on the day. In the deals above where the sources say so, the founder stayed on: Appel stayed on at Boston Calling, and Rotella said Insomniac was "still a stand-alone company" after its deal.563031 Where ownership does show up, it tends to be in four places.

      • Tickets. A festival owned by a company with its own ticketing business will usually sell through it. Live Nation's ticketing segment and CTS Eventim's ticket business are both reported in their accounts.154
      • Sponsors. Live Nation's annual report says "a large portion of sponsorships are usually associated with our outdoor venues and festivals" and describes "multi-festival branding opportunities."1 A brand that signs with a big owner can appear at several of its festivals at once.
      • Artist exclusivity. Large festivals use radius clauses, which limit where else an act can play around the festival. Owners say this protects a lineup they paid for; independent promoters have argued in court that it shuts them out. Where the money goes covers the terms and the cases.
      • Stability. A big owner can absorb a bad year more easily than an independent can. The flip side, critics argue, is less room for small rivals. Neil Young's complaint about Glastonbury shows the argument is about partners and influence as well as owners.55

      Supporters of consolidation point to money for production, safety and artist fees that small operators cannot match. Critics point to fewer independent buyers and ticket and sponsor tie-ins. This guide takes no side.

      04Where the money goes

      The short version: Tickets bring in most of a festival's money, with sponsorship, food and bar, camping and ticket fees on top. The biggest cost is the lineup, and because headliners are usually paid a fixed guarantee, the festival carries the risk if tickets do not sell. Headliner fees are rarely disclosed; the figures that circulate are press reports, such as a reported $3-4 million each for Coachella's 2017 headliners. Contracts also limit where artists can play nearby (the radius clause), insurance has become harder to buy since COVID, and the failures that make the news - Fyre, Woodstock 50, Astroworld - each failed in a different way.

      Where does a festival's money come from?

      The ticket is the main event, but it is not the only line. Live Nation's annual report for 2025 says that besides ticket sales, "we also earn revenue from the sale of concessions, camping fees and service charges earned on tickets sold."1 It adds that festival sites, although they "only host a few events each year," "can provide higher operating income because we are able to generate income from many different services." Its festivals range "from 10,000 to over 100,000 fans per day."1

      Sponsorship is the other big pillar. The same report says "a large portion of sponsorships are usually associated with our outdoor venues and festivals," and describes "multi-festival branding opportunities," pre-sales and onsite product activation.1 Sponsorship activation is the industry term for a sponsor's physical presence on site: a branded stage, a booth, free samples. Across the whole company in 2025, Live Nation reported Concerts revenue of $20.9 billion (83%), Ticketing $3.1 billion (12%) and Sponsorship and Advertising $1.3 billion (5%), with more than 1,500 sponsors and 159 million fans at its concert events.1 Those are company-wide figures, not festival-only ones.

      There is no audited public breakdown of one festival's revenue by line (tickets vs sponsors vs bar vs VIP).

      For what goes into the fees on a ticket checkout page, see the sister guide to tickets.

      How much money does Coachella make?

      The last public number is from 2017. Billboard reported that Coachella grossed $114.6 million over its two weekends that year, a festival franchise record.59 Grammy.com described the same figure as "profit," which looks like a misstatement of gross, since a gross is ticket revenue before costs.60 For scale, Coachella's 2007 edition brought in a reported $16 million with 62,000 attendees, and 2012, the first year with two weekends, brought in $47.3 million.5960 The one-off Desert Trip in 2016 reportedly topped $160 million.59

      With Coachella absent from the public chart, Pollstar's 2023 festival ranking was led by Outside Lands and HARD Summer, followed by Phish: Riviera Maya. Germany's Hurricane grossed a reported $20.1 million on 233,000 tickets, and the top eight together grossed $192 million.61

      Does Glastonbury make a profit?

      Yes, though less than its size suggests. Glastonbury's 2024 accounts show revenue of £68.4 million, up 20%, and a profit of £5.9 million, per Companies House filings reported by DJ Mag.50 A spokesperson credited "successful, dry Glastonburys in both 2023 and 2024" with rebuilding reserves after the "record losses" of the 2020 and 2021 cancellations.50

      The older accounts show how thin the margin can be. In 2013 the festival took £35 million in ticket sales but made £764,000 profit, and paid £2.6 million to Eavis-family companies for things such as land rental, staff costs and management fees. In 2012, a planned fallow year with no festival to rest the land, it lost £544,000.62

      What does a festival spend money on?

      Talent first. Live Nation's report says "artist fees are typically fixed guarantees for these events," which is why changes in festival revenue flow largely through to operating income.1 A guarantee is a fee promised to the artist whether or not tickets sell, so the festival keeps the upside of a sell-out and eats the loss of a slow year.

      The rest is site, staging, security, sanitation, insurance and marketing. The festival technology vendor Ticket Fairy estimates on its blog that artist fees are "often around 40% or more" of a budget, 50% or more at major festivals, with operational costs at 50-60% at mid-size events; those are a vendor's unsourced estimates, not audited figures.63

      Public land adds its own line. In Chicago, Lollapalooza paid royalties to the Parkways Foundation ranging from $400,000 (2005) to $1.5 million (2008), and its 2008 deal for Grant Park, running through 2018, barred rival multi-day, multi-stage festivals in the park.64

      Costs went up and stayed up after the pandemic. "Costs went up a lot in late '21, '22 into '23," Live Nation CFO Joe Berchtold told Bloomberg in 2024. Bloomberg counted at least 10 US festival cancellations in early 2024, among them Riverbend, Firefly (taking another year off), Okeechobee (on pause) and Flavortown (canceled before its first edition).65

      What is a radius clause?

      A radius clause is a non-compete in an artist's festival contract: the act agrees not to play within a set distance of the festival for a set period before and after it.69 Promoters say the clause keeps the lineup they paid for exclusive and stops rivals from free-riding on their booking. Critics, including independent promoters who have gone to court, say it lets a big festival shut smaller rivals out of acts far from its own site.70 Terms vary: one agency's sample clause is 100 miles (about 160 km), from 90 days before to 30 days after.71 For a fan, the practical effect is that an artist may skip your city's clubs for months around a festival booking; how a lineup gets made covers the rest of the offer.

      What happened in the Soul'd Out case?

      It is the main court test of a radius clause in the US. Soul'd Out, a festival in Portland, Oregon, sued AEG and Goldenvoice in spring 2018, saying Coachella's clause kept SZA and Tank and the Bangas off its 2018 bill.70 The complaint set out Coachella's terms as Soul'd Out described them: no other North American festival from December 15 to May 1, no shows in seven Southern California counties (Los Angeles, Orange, Riverside, San Bernardino, Santa Barbara, Ventura and San Diego), and no announcing other California shows until May 7.74 Soul'd Out's attorney told Willamette Week that Coachella was trying to "exert its market power over 1,000 miles away."70

      AEG argued the clause protects a curated lineup from free-riding. Judge Michael Mosman of the US District Court in Oregon dismissed the suit in October 2018 with leave to refile, then dismissed it again in March 2019, finding no antitrust injury - harm to competition as a whole, which a plaintiff has to show, rather than harm to one competitor.70 In May 2020 a Ninth Circuit panel (Judges Schroeder, Watford and Hurwitz) reversed, finding Soul'd Out had alleged a concrete injury even though it was not a party to the contracts.75 On August 19, 2020, Mosman let the case proceed into discovery.76

      The case ended that fall. On November 25, 2020, Digital Music News reported a court filing showing the suit "dismissed with prejudice," with each side bearing its own attorneys' fees; the outlet reported it as a settlement, and no reason was given.77

      Do festivals have cancellation insurance for weather?

      They can, though it has become harder to buy since COVID. Event cancellation (or contingency) insurance repays costs or lost income if a show is called off for a listed reason; festivals also carry liability cover for injuries and damage.

      The pandemic changed the market. Insurance Journal reported in 2022 that event-cancellation losses from COVID were about $6-8 billion, that cover for communicable disease was no longer available ("no option now to purchase it," said Christian Phillips of the broker Paragon), that some carriers had left the market and that placing 100% of limits for medium and large events was difficult. The broker Hub International forecast 2022 rate rises averaging 5% to 20% or higher, depending on the line of business.78 The result is a communicable disease exclusion in most policies: an outbreak is the festival's own risk.

      Weather works on the balance sheet even when nothing is canceled, as Glastonbury's "successful, dry" 2023 and 2024 show.50 Live Nation's annual report lists the seasonality of its festivals, and the risk of personal injury claims from incidents at festival sites it owns or rents, among its risk factors, naming Astroworld.1

      A weather claim can take a long time to resolve. Virginia's Blue Ridge Rock Festival was called off partway through in September 2023 after storms. In April 2024 the organizers' attorney, Jonathan Wilkofsky, described the claim as a multimillion-dollar one and told WDBJ7 "There's been no payment. There's been no denial of the claim," with the insurer still investigating.79 Organizers had tied ticket refunds to the claim, and on September 4, 2024 WSLS reported that "nothing has come from the festival itself."80 No final insurance outcome was found for this guide.

      What happened to Fyre Festival?

      Fyre was a fraud. It was planned for the Bahamas in April 2017. In October 2018, Judge Naomi Reice Buchwald sentenced founder Billy McFarland to six years in federal prison in Manhattan; prosecutors had asked for 15. US Attorney Geoffrey Berman said investors and customers lost more than $26 million across two schemes, Fyre and a later New York ticket scam called NYC VIP Access.8182

      The reports differ on details. NBC News says McFarland was ordered to forfeit $26 million, while Courthouse News calls it $26 million in restitution; NBC dates his Fyre plea to March 2018 and Courthouse News to July 2018.8182 The court record is the place to settle it.

      What happened to Woodstock 50?

      Woodstock 50 was planned for August 2019 at Watkins Glen, New York. In April 2019 its financier, Dentsu Aegis, withdrew more than $18 million without notice and announced the festival was canceled. Attempts to move it to Vernon Downs in New York and then Merriweather Post Pavilion in Maryland failed. An arbitration panel later found that Dentsu had breached the contract, and Dentsu settled for an undisclosed sum "covering damages but not unrealized profits."83 It is the clearest example of a festival failing at the financing stage rather than on site.

      What has Astroworld cost?

      Ten people died in a crowd crush at Astroworld in Houston in November 2021. Live Nation, the promoter, settled all 10 wrongful-death lawsuits, the last with the family of a nine-year-old victim on May 23, 2024, on undisclosed terms. About 2,400 injury claims were reported still pending at that point.84 In the first quarter of 2024, Live Nation recorded $186 million for "estimated probable losses in excess of our expected probable insurance recoveries" tied to Astroworld, according to an SEC filing reported by CMU.84 By October 23, 2024, more than 300 injury plaintiffs had settled with Live Nation and Travis Scott, with "hundreds" still pending, per ABC13 Houston.85 Live Nation's annual report for 2025 says "all lawsuits relating to Astroworld have been resolved since early 2025," with losses in excess of its insurance recovery.1 The crowd-safety lessons are in the safety section.

      05How a lineup gets made

      The short version: A lineup is a stack of signed offers. A festival's talent buyer sends written offers to booking agents, works inside a fixed talent budget, and times each one around where an artist's tour already goes. The poster that comes out months later is its own negotiated document: how high a name sits and how big its letters are tells you, roughly, how much the festival thinks that act sells. Some festivals rank by font size, some go alphabetical, and a few now make the order deliberately hard to read.

      Who actually books a festival?

      At BottleRock Napa Valley, nearly every offer goes out from one person: talent buyer Tom Hoppa of Latitude 38, working to a fixed talent budget set by the festival's partners (as described in 2025).86 The talent buyer is the festival-side person who chooses artists and makes them offers; the artist's side is represented by a booking agent.

      Bigger operations run more like a trading floor. Charles Attal, a co-founder of C3 Presents, the company behind Lollapalooza, described a seven-buyer booking department as "a big buying pit - everybody can hear everybody else" (in a Billboard interview from around 2008).87

      What the buyer weighs varies by festival, but BottleRock's checklist is typical of what gets reported: an artist's existing Bay Area dates, their national and international tours, album release timing and personal circumstances.86 That is routing - where an artist's tour already takes them. An act already passing through the region is cheaper and easier to book than one who would have to fly in for a single date.

      What is in an offer?

      An offer (or offer sheet) is a contract started by the buyer. It names the artist, the fee type, the logistics, and the deposit and payment schedule, and it "can become binding once accepted," according to DEGY Entertainment, an agency that books fairs and festivals.71

      The fee type matters. A flat guarantee is paid regardless of how tickets sell. DEGY also describes a split point deal: a base guarantee plus a percentage of ticket sales once the promoter's expenses are recouped.71 Most fans never see any of this; it shows up later as the order of names on the poster.

      Before an offer is signed, dates are often "held." The terms come from venue booking. Werklund Centre, a performing arts venue in Calgary, Canada, defines a first hold as going to the client who showed interest first and so "has the first right of refusal," and a second hold as the next in line. A second- or third-hold client can challenge by paying a deposit; the earlier holder then has two business days to confirm or release the date (per its rental FAQ, as of November 2026).88

      No festival in these notes publishes its talent budget, and this guide does not estimate one.

      How far ahead are headliners booked?

      Earlier than the poster suggests. At BottleRock, booking for next year starts while this year is still being booked, and the three headliner slots come first: "Those three spots are truly the most important part of the lineup, no matter what."86

      Not everyone works top-down. Attal said Lollapalooza booking heated up around late November and wrapped in January, with headliners done by the first week of February - and added, "I never book headliners first, unless they just happen to come in" (circa 2008).87

      Ticket Fairy, a ticketing vendor whose blog is marketing content rather than reporting, claims headliners often confirm one to two years ahead, quotes Suwannee Hulaween's Michael Berg saying some are locked "two or more years out," and puts international acts at 12 months or more for routing and visas, with local and regional acts often booked three to six months out.89 Treat those as practitioner estimates, not industry data.

      When a headliner drops out, the lineup gets rebuilt in public. The same vendor summary lists Florence + The Machine moving up to headline Glastonbury in 2015 after Foo Fighters canceled, Gorillaz replacing U2 there in 2010, and Pulp replacing The Stone Roses in 1995.89

      What is a radius clause?

      A radius clause bars an artist from playing public shows within a set distance of the festival for a set number of days before and after it; DEGY's sample clause is 100 miles (about 160 km), from 90 days before to 30 days after.71 For a fan, it can mean an artist skips your city's clubs for months around a festival booking. Reported terms, the court cases and both sides' arguments are in where the money goes.

      Why does one festival's announcement hold up another's?

      Because announcement order is in the contracts too. Coachella's artist contracts give it first right to announce in its region; artists also booked for California or neighboring-state festivals have to wait until after their Coachella set to announce those, and elsewhere they usually wait only for Coachella's lineup reveal (as reported in 2023).90 Coachella's own story lives in the sister guide to Coachella, and the sales side of announcement day is in how festivals sell out.

      In Boston

      In January 2023, Boston Calling, Bonnaroo and Sonic Temple had planned to announce on January 10, timed hours apart at the Foo Fighters' request. Coachella's reveal ran late, and because Boston Calling and Bonnaroo shared acts with Coachella - 070 Shake, Sofi Tukker and Knocked Out - agents had to clear the announcements with Goldenvoice, Coachella's promoter, first.90 A Boston festival waited on one in the California desert. That is the system working as designed.

      Why are some names bigger on the poster?

      Because billing - an act's position and type size - signals status, and status affects what an artist can charge next time. Agents negotiate it hard.

      Bonnaroo's Bryan Benson of AC Entertainment put the default plainly: "the standard model is the headliners on top," with developing local and regional acts at the bottom. Placement often tracks stage slot and ticket-selling power, similar genres get spread apart to show variety, and artists do call after the poster comes out to ask why they are lower.91

      Bookers interviewed by VICE in 2019 said they weigh regional ticket sales, fees, buzz and slot. The details get granular. One booker: "We've had years where we've made artist's names less legible in order to move them up a line." Agents sometimes ask for kerning changes so a short name takes up as much width as a long one, and one festival with two co-headliners alternated whose name came first on social media each day.92 VICE also reported that few artists have poster-approval clauses.92

      Coachella has kept a descending-font-size format since 2003, according to Wallpaper* (2026).93

      The same 2019 IFF panel argued the question out. Ian Evans of IME Music rejected A-Z billing because it hides where the big budgets went.       Why are lineup posters getting harder to read?

      Partly on purpose. Wallpaper* noted in June 2026 that III Points in Miami uses a circle layout designed by its own talent buyer, Santi Vidal, who explained: "If you create this format where it's ambiguous, it makes your life easier." Camp Flog Gnaw used a crossword in 2024; HARD Summer and Portola use graffiti-style layouts with no clear order.93

      The trade-off is the one Melt! ran into: a poster nobody can rank is a poster where nobody can find the headliner.94

      What is a day split?

      A day split is the lineup broken out by day, usually released after the main poster. In 2025 BottleRock released the full lineup first and the daily lineups a few days later, building each day around one headliner plus acts that share its audience.86 If you are buying a single-day ticket, the day split is the document that matters.

      How do you read a festival poster?

      • Top line, biggest type: the headliners - usually the acts the festival is counting on to sell tickets.91
      • Line position is negotiated: a name can be squeezed or spaced to move it up a line, so compare lines, not letter heights.92
      • Check for alphabetical tiers: if a row runs A-Z, the order inside it means nothing; the row itself is the rank (Roskilde-style).94
      • Poster rank is not set time: an act low on the poster may play a late slot that avoids the main-stage headliner.94
      • Wait for the day split: that tells you who shares a day with whom, and usually who the day was built around.86
      • Note what is missing: acts with regional exclusivity elsewhere may not be announced yet.90
      • Unranked layouts are a choice: a circle, crossword or graffiti poster usually means the festival chose not to rank.93

      Elsewhere in the world

      Europe is where the billing argument happens in public: the 2019 IFF panel in London put bookers from Roskilde and Melt! and agents on one stage.94 Tomorrowland in Belgium runs the biggest all-alphabetical list cited here.93 In the UK, Glastonbury's headliner replacements - Pulp in 1995, Gorillaz in 2010, Florence + The Machine in 2015 - show how a top line can change after the poster is out.89 In the US, BottleRock, Bonnaroo and Coachella are the cited examples of the ranked, headliners-on-top model.869193

      06How festivals sell out

      The short version: Festivals sell in layers: past buyers first, then sponsor cardholders, then everyone on the list, then the general on-sale, with the price stepping up each time a tier empties. The lineup reveal is the biggest marketing moment of the year, but the strongest brands now sell out before naming anyone - Newport Folk did it in under a minute in 2025. After that, the fight moves to resale, where festivals either lock tickets to a named person or warn fans off the secondary market.

      Why is the lineup announcement such a big deal?

      Because for most festivals it is the moment the year's sales get going, and it is timed around everyone else's (how a lineup gets made explains the contract side). Coachella announced in the first week of January from 2014 to 2020, then the second week after the 2020-21 cancellations, and other festivals traditionally follow (as reported in 2023).90

      A leak counts as damage. Charles Attal of C3 Presents, Lollapalooza's producer, on a leaked lineup around 2008: "It's hurtful to the festival and the city of Chicago." A leak forces a round of calls to agents and can cost the festival radio and TV bookings built around the reveal.87

      Boston Calling announced its 2025 lineup on Instagram on January 6, 2025, with a presale two days later on January 8 at 10am ET (reported).95 Two days between reveal and presale is the window for fans to decide.

      Can a festival sell out before announcing anyone?

      Yes, and it is no longer rare. Lollapalooza's three-day passes sold out in under three hours in 2013 before the lineup was out, which organizers called a first in festival history; they then moved the reveal up from April 9.96

      Tomorrowland sold 200,000 tickets in its Belgian presale in 18 minutes in 2025, a record (the previous year took 27 minutes), for 400,000 ticket holders across two weekends, July 18-20 and 25-27, 2025. IQ Magazine named Wacken Open Air, Primavera Sound, Green Man and Electric Picnic as other speedy sellouts.97

      In New England: Newport Folk with no lineup

      Newport Folk Festival 2025 (July 25-27, 2025) sold out on February 5, 2025, "in less than 60 seconds (closer to 1 second)," without announcing a single performer, according to Newport Buzz. Resale listings reportedly passed $1,000 within minutes.98

      Sales ran through DICE. Later buyers were sent to a waiting list, and the festival warned fans against "speculative tickets" on secondary sites, noting that it does not issue PDF tickets.99 A "speculative" listing is a seller offering a ticket they may not have yet. More on Newport is in the New England section.

      Face value was modest next to those listings. What's Up Newp reported 2025 prices of $286.34 for a three-day general admission pass, $224.54 for two days and $116.39 for a single day, all including fees, with children under 10 admitted free (reported, not an official price list).100

      A presale is a sale window before the general on-sale. A loyalty presale gives early, sometimes cheaper, access to people who bought or attended before. Codes usually go to whoever is already on file, so keep the contact details on your ticketing account current.

      What do "early bird" and "Tier 1" mean?

      They are the cheapest tickets, sold first and in limited numbers. Once a tier sells out, the price rises to the next one.101

      How does Glastonbury sell tickets?

      Glastonbury does almost everything differently, and its official page for 2027 lays it out.105

      • Register first. Everyone aged 13 or over on June 23, 2027, has to register individually before the sale. Each ticket carries the registered holder's photo, and security admits only the person pictured.
      • Pay a deposit. A £100 deposit per person at booking, with the £308 balance plus postage due between 9am BST April 1 and 11:59pm BST April 7, 2027 - £408 in all, including a £5 booking fee, plus £11.25 postage per order.
      • Buy six at most. The limit is six tickets per transaction.
      • No transfers. Tickets are "strictly non-transferable" to prevent touting (the UK word for scalping).

      Standard tickets went on sale on October 4, 2026 (RadioX), and when the official page was loaded on November 5, 2026, it carried the headline "Tickets for Glastonbury 2027 have now sold out."106105

      How do festival payment plans work?

      A payment plan (also sold as layaway) splits the ticket into a deposit and later installments. Glastonbury's deposit-then-balance model is the long-standing UK version.105 In the US, Outside Lands' 2026 three-day payment plans "start at $99 down" (official).101 Riot Fest offered three- or six-month plans in 2019 through Affirm, a third-party lender, chosen at Eventbrite checkout; its page gave no fees or missed-payment terms.107

      The terms that matter - fees, and what happens if a payment fails - live in each festival's help center. Read them before you commit, not after.

      Resale: face-value exchanges vs. open resale

      There are two broad models. Glastonbury ties the ticket to a named, photographed person and allows only official resale: returned and canceled tickets go back on sale in spring 2027.105 That resale runs on two dates in late April 2027 - a Thursday for coach-plus-ticket packages and a Sunday general sale - and See Tickets is the only official seller.106 A ticketing vendor's case study says Michael Eavis introduced photo registration in the mid-2000s "to stop ticket touting altogether," with resold tickets going only to other registered users at the standard price; the exact year is unconfirmed.108

      The other model uses a ticketer's waiting list and warns fans off third-party sites, as Newport Folk did through DICE in 2025.99 Where neither is in place, the gap fills fast: those $1,000-plus Newport listings appeared within minutes.98

      Face-value resale means official resale at the original price. If a festival runs one, it is the safe way to buy late.

      Do festivals use dynamic pricing?

      Dynamic pricing means prices that move live with demand during the sale. The festivals cited in this guide publish pre-set rising tiers instead, which is a different thing: the steps are announced, not calculated on the fly.101

      The flashpoint was a tour, not a festival. The UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) opened an investigation into Ticketmaster over the August 31, 2024, Oasis on-sale, looking at dynamic pricing and consumer law. Informed sources estimated 10-15% of 1.4 million tickets were dynamically priced, with a face value around £150 against an "In Demand" price around £355. Oasis said it was not aware dynamic pricing would be used. The CMA said "it should not be assumed that Ticketmaster has broken consumer protection law."109

      In the US, the Federal Trade Commission's rule, in force since May 12, 2025, requires live-event sellers to show the total price, including mandatory fees, up front. Dynamic pricing is still allowed if the total is disclosed at each step. Optional add-ons such as VIP, plus taxes and shipping, can be shown separately but must appear in the final price before checkout, and resale marketplaces are covered too.110 This is all-in pricing.

      How do festivals advertise?

      Mostly through the audience. In Eventbrite's 2015 Pulse Report (500-plus event professionals, covering 2014), 36% of festival organizers rated free social promotion their most effective tactic, against 20% for other events, and 28% chose word of mouth, against 19%. Under 10% chose email, against 31% for other events, and 6% ranked paid social first, against 3%. 76% expected their social media use to rise.111

      Fans report the same thing from the other side. In Eventbrite's 2019 Australian Music Fans Report (1,100-plus respondents), 41% heard about the festival they attended through social media, 22% through word of mouth and 11% through organizers' direct marketing.112

      The aftermovie - a polished recap video released after the event - is the festival's year-round ad. Tomorrowland's 2011 aftermovie, about half an hour long, had passed 70 million views by 2015, and the format spread from Gottwood to EDC; Mixmag's writer argued it had become formulaic and leaves out the heat and the mud.113 By the festival's own 2017 figures, Tomorrowland claimed 1.2 billion online touchpoints, 200 million-plus livestream viewers, 184 million unique people reached on Facebook, 355 million YouTube minutes viewed and 77 million Snapchat views.114

      Paid Meta (Facebook and Instagram) ads and retargeting are part of most campaigns, but no independent data on festival ad spend turned up for this guide. For free lessons on selling out a room with Meta ads, see Darkroom Ad School.

      Early tiers vs. late buyers

      Festivals push early buying because the wider market has gone the other way. Eventbrite's 2024 Music Edit found "57% of all tickets are purchased a week or less before the show date," and the average gap between purchase and show fell 26% from 2022 to 2024. That covers all music events, not festivals, and the sample size was not stated.115 Tiers, loyalty codes and payment plans are the festival's answer: reasons to commit months out.

      07Building a city in a field

      The short version: A big greenfield festival is a small town built from nothing and taken apart again: stages, power, water, toilets, phone masts, fences and roads, assembled over weeks to months. In the UK the reference manual for doing it safely is the Purple Guide, published by the Events Industry Forum; it is guidance, not law. The law sits elsewhere - licensing acts, fire rules, health and safety duties - and in the US it is mostly local, city by city and county by county. Boston Calling shows how a city festival changes its layout from year to year.

      How long does it take to build a festival?

      Longer than the festival lasts, by a wide margin. At Glastonbury 2019, construction of a single area - Block9's IICON stage - had begun nearly three months before Vice's June 2019 report, published just ahead of the festival. Its components arrived on site at the start of June for a late-June festival, more than 1,000 people worked on that one project, and it used about 6.5 km (4 miles) of scaffolding, according to Vice's reporting.117

      The phone network arrives early too. For the same 2019 festival, the carrier EE drove six temporary mobile sites onto the farm in mid-May, with 20 engineers working on them. Vice reported the festival's data use on EE's network had grown from 1 TB in 2013 to 54 TB in 2017, with over 80 TB expected for 2019.117

      Multiply that by every stage, bar, campsite and service road, and the build becomes the longest phase of the festival year. The load-out runs the same process in reverse, usually faster, because nobody is building anything to last.

      The main stage goes up early as well. For Glastonbury 2022 (June 22-26), Emily Eavis posted a photo of the Pyramid Stage getting its covering at the end of March, and by April 10 ITV reported it "almost fully dressed" - roughly 10 to 12 weeks before the gates opened.118

      What is the Purple Guide?

      The Purple Guide is the UK's main good-practice manual for outdoor music and other events. The Health and Safety Executive (HSE), the UK government's workplace safety regulator, first published it; "its publication was taken over by the Events Industry Forum in 2012."119 The Events Industry Forum is an industry body, not a government department.120

      The Guide says it "is not designed to be prescriptive," and that its job is to "highlight legal responsibilities and non-legislative good practice."119 Most chapters are behind a subscription ("Login or subscribe to read more"), and the Forum says subscription income, after admin costs, goes back to the industry as grants.120119

      Flintshire County Council in Wales describes the "New Purple Guide" as the replacement for the original HSE guide, known as HSG195. The council says plainly that the Guide "is not the law," carries a subscription charge, and covers the Licensing Act 2003, the Civil Contingencies Act 2004 and the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005.121

      In practice, "not the law" does not mean optional. Councils, licensing officers and safety advisers use the Guide as the shared reference point, so an event plan that ignores it has some explaining to do.

      The HSE edition traveled. Roskilde Festival in Denmark says that after its 2000 disaster it helped promote "The Event Safety Guide" (the HSE guide) and was among the first to implement it.122

      The HSE lineage goes back to 1993, when it published the Guide to health, safety and welfare at pop concerts and similar events. The second edition, retitled The Event Safety Guide (HSG195), followed in 1999.123 As of November 2026, a single-user Purple Guide subscription costs £25 plus VAT a year, with multi-user plans also offered.124

      How many toilets does a festival need?

      The UK starting point, quoted in the free preview of the Purple Guide's sanitation chapter for a typical local event, is "one female toilet per 100 attendees and one male toilet per 500 attendees plus one urinal per 150." The Guide adds that "more female toilets are needed than male toilets."120

      Pembrokeshire County Council publishes the ratios split by how long the gates are open, and refers organizers to British Standard BS 6465 Part 1 (2006) and the Purple Guide:125

      Gates open Women Men
      6 hours or more 1 toilet per 100 1 toilet per 500, plus 1 urinal per 150
      Under 6 hours 1 toilet per 150 1 toilet per 600, plus 1 urinal per 175

      These are starting points. Councils adjust for the crowd mix, alcohol, camping and event length, which is why a camping weekend needs more than a one-day city show of the same size.

      At scale the numbers get large. Vice's 2019 profile of Glastonbury's toilet contractor, Natural Event, reported about 13 build crew rising to about 100 staff once the gates open, and an estimated 1.3 million door openings over the festival. The same article gives the contractor's toilet count as both 1,111 and "11,000," so the exact fleet size is unclear from that source.117

      In the US, the numbers are set locally, usually by county health departments. Salt Lake County in Utah, for example, uses a mass-gathering permit table based on peak crowd size and hours on site, requires 40% more toilets when alcohol is served, and asks for at least one hand-wash station per 10 toilets (application form loaded November 2026).126

      Where does a festival's drinking water come from?

      The Purple Guide states: "It is essential to make sure that drinking water (aka potable water) is available on site," and singles out hot weather and sites without mains water.127 On a farm that means bowsers, tanks and temporary pipe runs; in a city park it usually means tapping into the municipal supply.

      Supply is only half of it. The other half is how quickly people can reach a tap. At Boston Calling 2024, attendees reported long waits at water refill stations, and organizers added more stations for 2025.128

      What powers a festival?

      Mostly generators, and increasingly generators running on something other than fossil diesel. For 2023, Glastonbury said "all generators across the festival site," including the Pyramid Stage, would run on "palm oil-free HVO fuel - made from waste cooking oil," a renewable diesel replacement; some areas ran on solar PV and battery hybrid systems, and a temporary wind turbine powered some market stalls.129 What HVO is, and how much it cuts emissions, is covered in the impact section.

      Power is planned in zones: each stage, bar row, campsite and production compound gets its own supply, so a failure in one area does not take down the rest. Generators also need refueling on site, which puts fuel trucks on the same service roads as everything else during the build and the show.

      The alternative is the grid. Powerful Thinking, a UK festival sustainability group, reports that Norway's Øya Festival (capacity 70,000) switched from diesel generators to grid power in 2010, and that the UK's Cambridge Folk Festival partly runs on mains power, saving 12,000-15,000 liters of diesel per event; it notes a new connection can be expensive, with costs sometimes shared by venues, councils or landowners.130 In March 2026, Festival Republic unveiled a permanent mains supply in London's Finsbury Park to power the stage and other key areas at its events there, which it called a first for large-scale outdoor events in the UK.131

      How are festival stages built?

      Big festival stages are temporary demountable structures: steel or aluminum frames, roofs and towers bolted together on site and taken down afterward. In the UK, the HSE points organizers to Institution of Structural Engineers guidance on temporary demountable structures for detailed loading calculations.132 Wind is the load that matters most outdoors; the safety section covers what happened at the Indiana State Fair in 2011 when a temporary roof failed.

      A stage is also a crowd-management decision. After 2000, Roskilde rebuilt its Orange Stage area with a larger, taller stage, zoned pits, and redesigned flow and escape routes.122 A taller stage means people further back can see, which takes pressure off the front.

      For the gear that goes on and around a stage - rigging, lighting, audio, video - the sister guide at https://showfile.cold.fyi/ goes deeper.

      Why is there a gap between the crowd and the stage?

      That gap is the pit, and the fence in front of it is the front-of-stage barrier. According to the HSE, the barrier is built on a load-bearing "A" frame in steel or aluminum, with sections "around 1200mm high and 1m wide" (about 4 feet by 3 feet). The audience stands on a footplate, which uses their own weight to hold the barrier down; staff work from a rear step. There should be "no access gates on pressured sections."132

      The HSE favors curved barrier layouts. A curve creates more distance to the stage, a wider pit for "stewards, first-aiders and paramedics," and helps in "dissipating audience surges away from the centre." The guidance warns against pockets where people can get trapped and calls for escape routes on both sides.132

      Large crowds are often split further. A finger barrier runs back from the front into the crowd, and the HSE says it must take the same load as the front barrier.132 A zoned pit system uses multiple barrier lines to divide the area in front of a big stage into pens. Roskilde introduced one at its Orange Stage and Arena after 2000, along with "systematic crowd monitoring" by trained observers.122

      In the US, the closest equivalents are ANSI-approved standards written by ESTA, the Entertainment Services and Technology Association. ANSI ES1.9, Event Safety - Crowd Management, was approved in July 2020 and covers the basic principles and vocabulary of crowd management.133 It was followed in 2023 by ES1.40, Event Safety - Security, and a revised E1.21 for temporary outdoor structures.134

      Does a festival need a license?

      Yes, though which ones depends entirely on where it is.

      In the UK, the Purple Guide covers the Licensing Act 2003 alongside health and safety, civil contingency and fire law.121 Licensing is handled by local councils, which is why so much practical event guidance in this section comes from council websites.

      Under the Licensing Act 2003 in England and Wales, every license decision turns on four objectives, which Gravesham Borough Council lists as "of equal importance": the prevention of crime and disorder, public safety, the prevention of public nuisance, and the protection of children from harm.135 Many councils also run a Safety Advisory Group (SAG) of council, police, fire, ambulance and highways officers. Surrey Heath's SAG, for example, advises organizers of large or unusual events, and its members "will not accept or adopt any of the responsibilities of the organiser."136

      In the US, there is no national festival license and no single national equivalent of the Purple Guide. Cities and counties issue the event, entertainment and alcohol permissions. After Astroworld, the Texas Task Force on Concert Safety (April 2022) recommended that municipal mass gatherings follow state permit guidelines, that local authorities stop events held without a permit or beyond its terms, and that the state produce a universal permit template and checklist for County Judges, plus an online Event Production Guide.137

      In Boston

      Boston Calling, held at the Harvard Athletic Complex in Allston since 2017 according to Wikipedia, is the city's worked example.9 It did not run in 2026. When organizers announced the hiatus in June 2025, they named June 4-6, 2027, for the next edition,9 but Axios reported in October 2026 that the return was in doubt as organizers stayed silent; the New England section has the details.138

      Its layout changes with experience. For 2025, the festival merged its Green and Red stages into a rotating stage system to improve sightlines and movement, published a revised site map, and added water stations after 2024 complaints about refill lines.128 Those changes followed the crowding on Sunday, May 26, 2024, covered in the safety section.

      On licensing, the City of Boston Licensing Board issued an advisory on August 23, 2024, reminding licensees that they may not hold events "outside the scope of their license without explicit approval from the Board," must follow closing hours, capacity and premises terms, and face penalties up to suspension or revocation.139 That advisory is aimed at licensed premises, but it shows the principle: in Boston, the license defines what you can do and where.

      Elsewhere in the world

      • UK: The Purple Guide (Events Industry Forum, since 2012) is the shared reference; Glastonbury is the best-documented build, from three-month stage builds to mid-May phone masts.119117
      • Denmark: Roskilde rebuilt its main stage area after 2000 and helped spread the HSE guide; Denmark published a national concert safety guide in 2002.122
      • US: Permits are local. Texas has gone furthest toward a state template, with its 2022 task force report and Event Production Guide.137

      08Crowds, heat and keeping people safe

      The short version: Most festival injuries are small - sprains, stomach trouble, blisters - and most of the safety system exists to keep it that way. The rare disasters have a few repeating causes: too many people in too little space, wind on temporary structures, heat, and plans that did not cover what happened. Each major incident left an official report, and those reports shaped today's rules on crowd density, barriers, unified command and weather stops. Drug checking is one of the most contested safety policies, and the law on it differs sharply between the UK, the US and Australia.

      How many people per square meter is dangerous?

      Crowd safety is measured in people per square meter (one square meter is about 10.8 square feet). The figures below are as given by crowd scientist G. Keith Still, who cites UK event and stadium guidance:140

      Density What it means
      2 per m² The level UK event safety limits are "typically assessed at"
      4 per m² Guidance figure for moving queues
      4.7 per m² The Green Guide (UK Guide to Safety at Sports Grounds) figure for standing viewing areas
      5 per m² "This should be the UPPER limit for standing/viewing spaces"

      Above the upper limit, people stop choosing where they move. Pressure travels through the crowd in waves, which is why investigators have described some disasters as crowd turbulence rather than a "stampede." The Wikipedia account of the 2010 Love Parade disaster reports a 2012 analysis that used exactly that distinction.141 In a crush, the usual cause of death is compression asphyxiation: being squeezed so hard that breathing stops. That was the ruling for all 10 deaths at Astroworld in 2021.142

      One caution on sources: Still's expertise has been challenged in court. The German court in the Love Parade trial called his expert report "unusable," according to Wikipedia; critics said it ignored possible police mistakes.141 The density figures here are attributed to him and to the guides he cites, not presented as universal law.

      What happened at Roskilde in 2000?

      On June 30, 2000, nine young men from five countries died in a crowd crush near the front of the Orange Stage at Roskilde Festival in Denmark, during Pearl Jam's set.122 A case-study compilation by Working With Crowds gives the surrounding detail: about 50,000 people at the set, heavy rain beforehand, the band on around 10:30 p.m., victims aged 17 to 26, and 26 people injured, 3 seriously.143

      The festival's own committee reported in December 2000. Denmark published a national concert safety guide in 2002.122 A Danish police report to parliament found the band "morally responsible," as reported by ABC News citing NME; Pearl Jam defended its conduct.143

      The physical changes are still visible. Roskilde rebuilt the Orange Stage area with a larger, taller stage, zoned pits and redesigned escape routes, and introduced systematic crowd monitoring by trained observers.122 A Memorial Grove of nine birch trees stands on the site.122

      What happened at the Love Parade in 2010?

      The Love Parade disaster in Duisburg, Germany, killed 21 people (13 women and 8 men, aged 18 to 38) and injured 652 on July 24, 2010. Unless the court is named as the source, the facts in this item come from Wikipedia's account and are attributed to it.141

      According to that account, it was the first time the event was held in a closed-off area, a former freight yard. A 240 m (about 790 ft) tunnel led to a ramp that served as the main entrance and exit. The area's maximum capacity was 250,000; claims about attendance ranged from 200,000 to 1.4 million. About 3,200 police were deployed.141

      A criminal trial opened on December 8, 2017, against six city officials and four staff of the organizer, Lopavent. Charges against seven were dropped in February 2019. The trial ended in May 2020 without a verdict, with the court citing minor culpability and the 10-year statute of limitations.141

      The Duisburg Regional Court's own press release, dated May 4, 2020, says its 6th Criminal Chamber discontinued the case against the three remaining defendants under section 153 of the German Code of Criminal Procedure, with the consent of the defendants and prosecutors, after 183 days of hearings.144 The court said any individual guilt was "gering" (minor), because the defendants' actions made the disaster possible only together with many other circumstances, and it pointed to the near 10-year length of the case and the strain on the bereaved and injured co-plaintiffs. The press release itself does not mention the statute of limitations.144

      What happened at Astroworld in 2021?

      Ten people, aged 9 to 27, died of compression asphyxiation during Travis Scott's set at Astroworld, at NRG Park in Houston, on November 5, 2021.142145 Capacity was reported as 50,000.146 About 300 people were treated on site and 25 taken to hospitals.142

      Houston police released their final report, 1,266 pages long, on July 28, 2023.145142 It found that the 56-page event operations plan covered shootings, bomb threats and weather, but not crowd surges. A grand jury returned six "no-bills," declining to indict Scott and five others.142 Scott pledged almost $5 million to an event safety initiative in 2022.145

      Texas Governor Greg Abbott launched the Texas Task Force on Concert Safety on November 10, 2021, led by Texas Music Office Director Brendon Anthony. Its report, released April 19, 2022, recommended:137

      • a Unified On-Site Command at events, bringing in the EMS, fire and police agencies that would answer a 911 call
      • attendee codes of conduct in ticketing
      • venues consulting other venues that have hosted the same artist
      • local authorities stopping unpermitted events or events exceeding their permit
      • a universal permit template for County Judges, and an online Event Production Guide

      Why did the Indiana State Fair stage collapse?

      The structure failed in wind it should have withstood under code, and the ballast holding it down was inadequate, according to engineering reports released in April 2012.

      On August 13, 2011, a temporary stage roof collapsed at the Indiana State Fair in Indianapolis before a concert. Seven people died, five at the scene, and dozens were injured.147

      Those reports, as summarized by CNN, set out the detail. The National Weather Service estimated gusts of 60-70 mph (about 97-113 km/h). Engineers put the wind at the moment of failure near 59 mph (about 95 km/h), below the 68 mph (about 109 km/h) minimum the structure should have withstood under code. The engineering firm Thornton Tomasetti found the ballast - concrete Jersey barriers holding the structure down - "grossly inadequate." A separate review by Witt Associates found inadequate emergency planning.147

      The lesson the industry took is in two parts: an engineer checks the structure and its ballast against wind, and the event has a written plan saying at what wind speed the show stops and who makes that call.

      How many people end up in the medical tent?

      Roughly 13 to 17 people per 1,000, going by two peer-reviewed studies, and only a small fraction of them go to hospital. At Glastonbury 2022 that worked out to about 1 person in 75 needing some care and about 1 in 3,300 going to hospital.

      The Glastonbury figures come from a study in the journal Prehospital and Disaster Medicine. Of 210,000 people on site (143,000 ticket holders plus 67,000 staff, volunteers and performers), 2,828 were seen by medical services - a patient presentation rate of 13.47 per 1,000. Sixty-four went to hospital (62 by road, 2 by air), a rate of 0.30 per 1,000. The top diagnoses were joint injuries, gastrointestinal problems and blisters. The service ran on 832 medical volunteers, 533 of them clinicians.148

      North American figures are in the same range. A study in the same journal (published December 2018) of four large multi-day North American music festivals over two years counted 10,406 patient encounters across 34 event days, a presentation rate of 16.5 per 1,000, and a transfer-to-hospital rate of 0.25 per 1,000 including non-ambulance transport.149

      City festivals lean on city services. At Boston Calling 2024, Boston EMS logged "close to 800" medical calls over three days, with 23 people transported.128

      What happens when the weather turns?

      Stages close, crowds are told to shelter in place, and sometimes the festival is called off. Wind and rain each have recent cases; heat has its own item below.

      • Wind, Belgium, 2011: On Thursday, August 18, 2011, a storm hit Pukkelpop in Kiewit, near Hasselt, in about 10 minutes, with about 60,000 on site by the organizer's estimate. Tents collapsed and trees and scaffolds fell.150 Five people died; the festival was put on hold, then canceled the next day. Overloaded phone networks slowed emergency services, which later built their own network for internal communication.151
      • Wind, Las Vegas, 2025: On Saturday, May 17, 2025, winds of 20-30 mph gusting above 40 mph (about 64 km/h) closed several stages and all rides at EDC Las Vegas; most reopened around 10:45 p.m.152
      • Rain, Nevada, 2023: Burning Man (August 28 - September 4, 2023) received up to 0.8 inches (about 20 mm) of rain in 24 hours, two to three months' worth. Participants were told to shelter in place, the gate and airport closed, and only emergency vehicles moved. More than 70,000 people typically attend. One death was under investigation by the Pershing County Sheriff.153

      A shelter in place order means stay where you are rather than try to leave - on a playa of mud, the safer choice.

      What did Woodstock '99 teach festivals about heat?

      That drinking water has to be easy to get when it is hot, a point today's UK guidance makes directly.

      Woodstock '99 opened July 23, 1999, at the former Griffiss Air Force Base in Rome, New York, with "some 250,000" people, according to HISTORY.com. The tarmac reached "over 100 degrees Fahrenheit" (about 38 C), water bottles cost "$4 apiece," hundreds were treated for heatstroke and dehydration, and three people died.154

      The Associated Press, reporting on July 25, 1999, gave "90-degree-plus" temperatures (above 32 C) and said nearly 1,000 had been treated for heat exhaustion and dehydration, citing promoter John Scher. Scher said: "There's lots of free water here," pointing to sprinkler "rain rooms."155 The two accounts disagree on how easy water was to get, and both are on the record.

      The Purple Guide, the UK's event safety guidance, says "It is essential to make sure that drinking water (aka potable water) is available on site," with hot weather singled out.127

      Heat is still a working problem in the US. At Bonnaroo in Tennessee in June 2024, The Tennessean reported an average high of 93 F (about 34 C), heat indexes in the triple digits, and medical tents across the campgrounds treating heat-related illness throughout the weekend; it gave no case count.156

      What can I bring, and why are bag rules so strict?

      Rules vary by festival and change by year, so the current official FAQ is the only reliable answer. Boston Calling shows how much they move. In 2018, Boston 25 News reported a 12 x 12 inch bag limit, small clear plastic bags allowed, no backpacks, metal detectors at the entrance, and a banned list that included weapons, illegal substances, tobacco and lighters, squirt guns and misters, laser pens, professional cameras, cans and canteens, chairs, strollers, bikes, umbrellas, totems, walkie-talkies and pets.157 By 2023, WBUR reported a bag limit of under 6 x 9 inches, with one factory-sealed plastic water bottle, an empty reusable bottle or an empty hydration pack allowed.158 The full 2023 list is in the gate item in Wristbands, cashless and the gate.

      Security tightened after the May 2017 Manchester Arena attack. Boston Calling's first year in Allston came days later: Boston police and the contractor RSIG Security inspected the grounds, all bags were checked, and bomb technicians and dogs swept the site. Police Commissioner Bill Evans said: "We'll step up the visibility, the number of officers."159

      In the UK, gate security is licensed. Guidance from the Security Industry Authority (May 2022) says searching people or property to keep out unauthorized or illegal items is licensable work under the Private Security Industry Act 2001, as is guarding; in licensed areas, where alcohol or regulated entertainment is provided, it needs a door supervisor licence.

      Stewarding - directing people to seats, toilets or first aid - does not need a licence, and neither do unpaid volunteers.160 (How festival volunteering works is in Waste, carbon and the town next door.) UK festival lists focus on items more than bag sizes: Radio X's guide to Glastonbury 2025 lists bans on fireworks and sky lanterns, knives, drones, lasers, glass, personal generators and pets other than assistance dogs, and notes that police search for drugs and confiscated items are not returned.161

      Is drug checking legal at festivals?

      It depends on the country, and in Australia on the state: drug checking is legal in some places, restricted in others, and argued over everywhere. Drug checking (often called pill testing) is the chemical testing of a substance to tell the person who handed it in what it contains. This guide reports the policy and both sides; it gives no advice on use. For health information, go to DanceSafe in the US, The Loop in the UK, or your local official health services.

      UK. In 2023 the Home Office introduced a last-minute licence requirement for on-site testing, reported to cost over £3,000, and The Loop, the UK nonprofit that runs festival drug checking, could not operate at Parklife that year.       09Wristbands, cashless and the gate

      The short version: The band on your wrist is mostly a number. The chip inside carries a unique ID that the festival looks up in its own database every time you tap or walk through a gate, which is why activating it online matters and why a cut band is a dead band. Many festivals now run the bar, the food trucks and the merch tent off that same chip, and the leftover money comes back only if you ask for it online, inside a window, usually minus a small fee. The gate is where all of this meets a bag check, a scanner and, if you are unlucky, a counterfeit.

      What is actually inside a festival wristband?

      A small radio chip and an antenna, and the chip's main job is to say one number. Intellitix, a festival RFID vendor, says the chip holds a unique identifier (UID) and "does not hold any personal information"; the scanner reads the UID and checks it against a secure database where your ticket, your name and any payment details live.172 RFID (radio frequency identification) is the umbrella term: no battery, powered by the reader's own radio field when it comes close.

      Two radio families show up at festivals, and they do different jobs:

      Type Frequency Typical read range What festivals use it for
      HF / NFC (near field communication) 13.56 MHz about 4 cm (1.5 in) Deliberate taps: paying at a bar, topping up, VIP lanes
      UHF (ultra high frequency) UHF band about 1-6 m (3-20 ft) in standard setups Gates: several bands read at once, timestamped, hands-free
      Dual-frequency Both Both One band that does gates and payments

      Figures from Wristband.com, a wristband seller (May 2026 guide).173 The short NFC range is a feature: you do not want a bar terminal charging the person behind you. The long UHF range is also a feature: a gate can read a crowd walking through without anyone stopping to tap.

      Ticket Fairy, a ticketing vendor, describes the reads as encrypted challenge-response exchanges rather than a plain number being shouted into the air, and says readers and payment terminals can work offline, caching transactions until the network comes back.174 That offline mode matters more than it sounds - see the phone section below.

      Do festival wristbands track your location?

      According to the vendors and festivals that run them, no. Intellitix says bands are scanned only at marked access points and do not communicate location after entry or exit.172 splash! Festival in Germany says flatly that its chips have no GPS.175 What a band does leave is a trail of scans: every gate you passed and, on a cashless site, every purchase. splash! is candid about this, saying its transactions are recorded and the system is therefore "not fully anonymous."175 So the honest version: a wristband knows where you were each time you tapped it, not where you are.

      How do I activate my festival wristband, and why bother?

      Activation means registering the band's ID online so the festival's database ties it to you. Hinterland in Iowa, in festival information it says is from its 2026 edition, asks attendees to activate before arriving, link a payment card if they want to pay cashless, and add an emergency contact.176

      The practical payoff is the lost band. Because the money and the ticket live in the database rather than on the chip, a registered band can be frozen online, according to Intellitix, and splash! says a registered chip can be blocked and its balance moved to a new one.172175 An unregistered band that falls off in a field is, to the system, a stranger's band with your money on it.

      Activation also sets up re-entry rules. Hinterland requires an exit scan for same-day re-entry.176 Ticket Fairy describes the general logic: a gate marks a band "used" on the way in and will not let it in again until it has been scanned out.174 Wristband.com calls this anti-passback - the gate flags a repeat entry with no exit between.173 It is the reason you cannot walk in, hand your band through the fence, and have a friend walk in on it.

      Why are some festival wristbands impossible to take off?

      Because the lock only goes one way. SeatGeek's explainer says fabric bands have a locking mechanism that cannot be removed without damage, and most use a one-way lock: once fastened, the band cannot be loosened.177 Pull it tighter and that is where it stays. SeatGeek adds the warning every first-timer learns the hard way: tighten it too far and you may have to cut it, which can invalidate it.177

      Festivals write this into the terms. Hinterland's wristband policy says the band must be worn so it "cannot be slipped on or off," that removing, stretching or tampering "will void entry," and that the band is a "revocable license" rather than something you own outright.176 Intellitix says a broken-off band cannot be reattached.172

      The design goal is one band, one wrist, for the whole weekend. A band that could slide off could be sold on the walk home on Friday night or passed back over a fence. The chip handles the digital side of that problem; the lock handles the physical side.

      Inside, one common design is a barrel lock with prongs. ID&C, a UK wristband supplier, says its fabric barrel lock uses "a one-way sliding mechanism with internal prongs that pierce the fabric," so that once fastened the band "can't be removed without cutting."178

      What happens if I cut my festival wristband?

      You will probably need a new one, and it may cost you. Intellitix says damaged bands are replaced at the box office with photo ID, and a fee may apply.172 Hinterland's policy is specific: one replacement only, $25, and only for the original purchaser showing photo ID.176 Removing the band yourself counts as tampering under that policy and voids entry.176 If a band is too tight to bear, go to the box office or the festival's help desk before reaching for scissors.

      How to spot a fake festival wristband (and who gets caught selling them)

      The fakes are real, and so are the arrests. The Las Vegas Review-Journal reported two men arrested over a counterfeit wristband scheme at Electric Daisy Carnival in Las Vegas, with bands ordered from an out-of-state supplier and an estimated value of $1 million; charges included obtaining money under false pretenses, attempted forgery and conspiracy.179 In Miami, WSVN 7News reported in March 2025 that a man was arrested for allegedly selling two fake Ultra Music Festival wristbands for $1,200 cash, charged with an organized scheme to defraud.180

      The EDC arrests date from the festival's June 2011 edition: the Las Vegas Sun reported them on June 25, 2011, saying the event organizer had alerted police to about $1 million in fake "all-access" wristbands.181

      The countermeasure is the chip. A printed copy of a band can look perfect and still fail at the gate, because the scanner is checking for a valid ID in the database, not for the right shade of fabric. Ticket Fairy claims Coachella "virtually eliminated counterfeit tickets at its festival gates" with RFID; that is a vendor claim and not independently verified.174 Festivals back the tech with policy: Hinterland says counterfeit or duplicated bands "will be confiscated by security" and tickets from unauthorized sources will not get in.176

      For the buyer, the takeaway is dull and reliable: buy from the festival or its official resale channel. Nobody outside a gate can show you that a band's chip is live.

      Why is there no phone signal at festivals?

      Because a field of 100,000 people is a small city that appears for three days, on land built for a few dozen. Carriers answer with COWs (cells on wheels) - portable cell towers on trucks or trailers - and specialist antennas.

      The data shows how hard the load climbs.       10Camping, getting there and getting home

      The short version: The ticket gets you in; the tent, the train and the ride home are the rest of the weekend, and they are where most of the cost and most of the stories come from. In the UK the signature camping problem is the abandoned tent - about 250,000 a year by the Association of Independent Festivals' estimate - while Glastonbury says 99% of its tents went home in 2022 and 2024. Rural festivals run shuttles from the nearest station and city festivals push transit and ban parking. Bring earplugs, find the free water, register for access support before you go, and budget for the whole trip, not the ticket.

      What happens to tents left at festivals?

      A lot of them stay behind. The Association of Independent Festivals (AIF) estimates that about 250,000 tents are abandoned at UK festivals each year, according to The Conversation, which adds that a typical 3.5 kg (7.7 lb) tent contains as much plastic as roughly 8,750 straws or 250 pint cups.189 The article is undated in our copy; its ID suggests 2019, so treat the 250,000 as an older, often-repeated estimate rather than a fresh count.

      The comforting story is that left tents go to charity. NME, reporting in 2018, called that "a bit of a myth": charities typically collect at most about 100 tents at a large festival.190 The same piece cites Powerful Thinking's estimate that UK festivals produce 23,500 tonnes of waste a year, two-thirds of it sent to landfill.190

      Festivals have tried carrots and deposits. NME reported that Bestival ran a compulsory £10 eco-bond, refunded when you handed in a bag of litter.190 The AIF's campaign aimed at retailers selling "festival tents" is in Waste, carbon and the town next door.

      How did Glastonbury get 99% of tents taken home?

      By asking, loudly, every year, and by making it part of the festival's identity. The slogan is "Love the farm, leave no trace."191 In 2019, more than 99% of tents were reported taken home, the same year Glastonbury ran its first full ban on selling single-use plastic bottles.191192

      The number has held. For 2022, Emily Eavis said about 99% of tents went home from roughly 210,000 attendees, with "only a few thousand" left behind, Radio X reported.193 As the 2024 festival ended (it closed on 1 July), she posted on Instagram that "99% of all tents have been taken home," the third year running at 99%, according to Mixmag.192

      Two caveats. The 99% is the organizer's own figure, reported by the press, not an independent audit. And 1% of a city-sized crowd is still a field of tents: "a few thousand" in 2022, as Radio X put it.193 Even so, against a national estimate of a quarter of a million, Glastonbury's numbers stand out.

      How do I get to Glastonbury by train?

      Ride to Castle Cary, then take the festival's free shuttle. National Rail's 2024 guide says Castle Cary is the nearest station and a free shuttle bus runs from there to the festival coach station at Pedestrian Gate A; you need your festival ticket to board.194 In 2024 the shuttles ran from Wednesday 26 June to Monday 1 July, with the last return buses timed to meet the final trains, and advance rail tickets went on sale up to 12 weeks ahead.194

      That pattern - rail to the nearest station, a ticket-holder shuttle for the last few miles - is the standard answer for big rural festivals. The hard part is the way out. Arrivals spread across several days; departures crowd into one morning, when every tent comes down at once and everyone wants the same trains. Glastonbury posts guidance for that day; on 29 June 2025 it published "Heat advice for Monday departures."195

      Most people still drive. Jam Packed, a UK festival audience travel study published by Julie's Bicycle in May 2009, found that in summer 2008, 72% of goers at greenfield festivals and 68% at peri-urban festivals arrived by car, with the rest split fairly evenly between coach and train; it surveyed more than 1,200 festival goers and counted occupancy in at least 1,700 cars at 8 festivals.196 That is an old figure, but it is the clearest published mode split found here.

      How do you get to a city festival with no parking?

      Transit, a bike where allowed, or your feet. City sites are usually parks and sports grounds with no room for cars. Boston Calling is a clean example - see the Boston section below.

      Do earplugs really work at concerts?

      Yes, and there is a randomized trial to show it. In a study reported in 2016, researchers at University Medical Center Utrecht in the Netherlands put 51 adults at an outdoor festival for about 4.5 hours, with sound averaging around 100 dB. Among those without earplugs, 40% had tinnitus or a temporary threshold shift (short-term hearing loss after loud noise); in the earplug group, 12% did, Inverse reported.197

      That is a big drop for a few dollars of foam or a pair of filtered musician's plugs. "Temporary" is the operative word in the study; repeated exposure is the long-term concern.

      Some countries cap the audience side, not only the neighbors' side. In France, a decree signed in August 2017 lowered the limit for amplified music to 102 decibels averaged over 15 minutes (from 105), extended the rules to open-air festivals, and required organizers to warn the audience, hand out free hearing protection and provide rest areas or quiet periods, according to the French environment ministry.198

      Can I bring a water bottle to a music festival?

      Usually yes, with conditions: typically one sealed bottle or an empty reusable one, as in Boston Calling's 2023 rules in Wristbands, cashless and the gate.158 An empty bottle is only useful if there is somewhere to fill it, which is where free water comes in.

      Water is a safety issue first. In the UK, the Purple Guide - published by the Events Industry Forum as "industry guidance" - says "It is essential to make sure that drinking water (aka potable water) is available on site," and singles out hot weather and sites without mains water.127 Ticket Fairy, a vendor, cites a planning rule of thumb of about 14 liters (3.7 gallons) per attendee over a weekend for drinking and basic washing, attributed to "industry experience" rather than a study, and says some regions mandate free drinking water at large events, pointing to the Northern Territory in Australia.199

      The cautionary history is Woodstock '99, where water was reportedly sold at $4 rising to $12, according to the same secondary source; the heat story is in Crowds, heat and keeping people safe.199 Twenty years later, Glastonbury 2019 banned single-use plastic bottle sales and provided free taps and WaterAid kiosks instead.199191 Free water is now a sustainability policy and a heat-safety policy at the same time.

      Heat cases can run into the hundreds. At CMA Music Festival in Nashville in June 2015, Vanderbilt University Medical Center reported treating 825 people for heat-related problems over the festival, more than in 2014, NewsChannel 5 reported.200

      How does accessibility work at music festivals?

      It depends heavily on the country and the festival, but the common shape is: register in advance, get a wristband for yourself and a companion, and use dedicated viewing areas, toilets and parking.

      In the UK, the charity Attitude is Everything has set the standard since 2000. It launched its Charter of Best Practice that year, signed its first 10 venues in the pilot's first year and reached 100 in 2014, and introduced the Live Events Access Charter in 2022; Glastonbury holds Gold status.201 In 2005 the charity started its Disabled Stewards Festivals Programme, putting disabled volunteers in steward roles.201 Its charter page says over 210 venues and events have gained awards to date; the page does not date that figure.202

      In North America, festivals run their own programs, and the details vary in ways that matter. AFAR reported in March 2026 that Coachella's viewing platforms restrict disabled attendees to a single companion and its Access+ program requires a pre-event application; Lollapalooza charges for companion tickets; SXSW gives complimentary access to a support person; and Ottawa Bluesfest added accessible lavatory RVs with adult changing tables.203 Companion policy and fees are a recurring point of criticism, per the same report.203

      11Waste, carbon and the town next door

      The short version: A festival is a temporary town, and it leaves the same marks a town does: carbon from getting there and keeping the lights on, tonnes of waste, noise over the fence, and money in local tills. The best-known UK estimate (2015) says audience travel is "up to 80%" of festival emissions; a 2023 study of measured events put the average at 41%, with a range of 18% to 76%. Generators are moving from diesel to HVO, a renewable diesel whose green credentials are contested. The economic impact numbers festivals publish are real estimates, usually commissioned, and rarely comparable from one event to the next.

      How big is a festival's carbon footprint?

      The number most people quote comes from The Show Must Go On, a report published in November 2015 by Powerful Thinking, a UK industry consortium. It estimated that UK festivals emit about 100,000 tonnes of CO2e (carbon dioxide equivalent, every greenhouse gas converted into one carbon number) a year once audience travel is counted, and about 20,000 tonnes from the sites themselves.209 The benchmarks were supplied by Julie's Bicycle, a UK climate charity for the creative sector that also runs Powerful Thinking's secretariat, with De Montfort University and Oxford's Environmental Change Institute as research partners.210 The consortium's founders were festival-side: Bestival, Festival Republic, A Greener Festival, the Association of Independent Festivals (AIF), Kambe Events and Firefly Solar.210

      On site, the same report found energy was about 65% of the footprint and waste about 35%. UK festivals were burning about 5 million liters (roughly 1.3 million US gallons) of diesel a year and producing about 23,500 tonnes of waste, with typical recycling rates likely below 32% (all 2015 estimates).209

      The report launched on 26 November 2015 with a pledge: festivals were asked to cut greenhouse gas emissions 50% by 2025.210 That pledge became Vision: 2025. Over 100 events had signed by 2020, and a second edition of the report followed.211

      No US-wide festival carbon benchmark turned up in this research, so the UK figures are the reference point.

      What is HVO, and why are festivals switching to it?

      HVO (hydrotreated vegetable oil) is a renewable diesel made from waste oils and fats that runs in ordinary diesel generators without modification. That drop-in quality is why festivals like it: the generator fleet stays the same, the fuel changes.

      Glastonbury 2023 (June 21-25) ran "all generators across the festival site," including the Pyramid Stage, on HVO made from used cooking oil, described as palm-oil free, with a claimed CO2 reduction of up to 90%. Production areas were to draw electricity from low-impact sources or solar-battery systems, and a temporary wind turbine was to power some market stalls (pre-festival plans).129 In Hull, Humber Street Sesh switched all its stages, bars and lighting from diesel to HVO in 2023; the supplier estimated a drop from 3,302 kg to 46 kg of carbon emissions.213

      The catch comes from Transport and Environment, a European clean-transport advocacy group. In a January 30, 2026 piece, it said HVO's top feedstocks are now used cooking oil, palm oil mill effluent and animal fats; that certification is hard to verify for supply from China, Indonesia and Malaysia; that a whistleblower alleges virgin palm oil has been mislabeled as sludge; and that in the best case HVO "only halves" CO2 compared with diesel once indirect emissions are counted.214

      So the claims run from "up to 90% lower" (suppliers and festivals) to "only halves" in the best case (T&E). No independent lifecycle audit of any single festival's HVO turned up here. The cleaner long-term option, where a site has it, is a grid connection or batteries and solar.

      No US festival running its whole site on HVO turned up in this research. The nearest US example goes the battery route: Lollapalooza in Chicago powered its main stage with a hybrid battery system in 2024 and brought it back in 2025 with over 1.5 MWh of storage, cutting how long its biodiesel generators ran. Live Nation, the festival's promoter, claimed the 2024 system cut main stage fuel use 67%, saved more than 3,000 gallons and avoided 26 metric tons of greenhouse gas emissions (company figures, July 2025).215

      Why did Glastonbury stop selling plastic bottles?

      Because it was selling a lot of them. Glastonbury sold over 1 million single-use plastic drinks bottles in 2017, and announced on 27 February 2019 that none would be sold on site or backstage at the 2019 festival.216 In their place: canned soft drinks and canned water, free drinking water at all bars, "hundreds of free water taps," and three times as many WaterAid kiosks. Attendees could still bring their own bottles.216

      The same post put numbers on the recycling side: about 45 tonnes of aluminum cans and about 40 tonnes of plastic bottles recycled at the previous festival.216

      No published audit of how much plastic the 2019 change removed turned up in this research.

      How do reusable cups work at Roskilde?

      Roskilde, in Denmark, runs one of the cleanest deposit systems in the business. Your first cup costs 5 DKK (under one US dollar). You can swap it freely for a beer, drink or coffee cup at the bar. When you hand a cup back, you get 1 DKK; the other 4 DKK pays for running the system. Cups can be stored on your wristband, up to 12 at a time. The system was introduced in 2019, and about 2 million cups go through it per festival (official figures).217

      A deposit cup works because the cup is worth something on the ground, so dropped cups get picked up. Deposit cups are common at festivals in northern Europe and much less common at US events.

      What happens to tents left at festivals?

      Most go to landfill, by the estimate of AIF, the UK's independent festivals trade body; its 2019 figure of 250,000 a year, and how Glastonbury gets 99% of tents taken home, are in Camping, getting there and getting home.218 AIF's "Take Your Tent Home" campaign launched on 8 May 2019 and asked retailers, including Tesco and Argos, to stop marketing cheap tents as "festival tents," a label that implies single use.218

      This is mostly a camping-festival problem. City day festivals, which describes most big US events, do not generate it at the same scale.

      No count of abandoned tents at US festivals turned up. At Bonnaroo in Tennessee, the Chattanooga Times Free Press reported in 2018 that a local outdoor resale shop, the Gear Closet, sends a cleanup crew to collect usable tents, tarps and other gear left in the fields; in 2017 it took about 4 tons of the festival's more than 500 tons of waste.219

      Why does Glastonbury take a year off?

      Glastonbury happens on a working dairy farm, and the farm needs its fields back. Emily Eavis confirmed 2026 as a fallow year - a year the festival skips - to "give the land a rest," adding that "it gives the cows a chance to stay out for longer and reclaim their land."220 She also said the break gives the audience time off and keeps the festival from being seen as "cashing in."220 So no, there is no Glastonbury in 2026; the history of its fallow years is in The global festival season.

      Most festivals on parks or fairgrounds face a different limit: a cap on how many days a year the land can be used for events (see Brockwell Park below).

      Can you rent your house out during a festival?

      How loud can a festival be near homes?

      In the UK, the reference is the 1995 Noise Council Code of Practice on Environmental Noise Control at Concerts, known as the Pop Code. As summarized in a 2011 Edinburgh Napier University report for Defra, it sets music noise targets measured 1 meter from the facade of nearby homes, averaged over 15 minutes (LAeq, in dB(A), decibels weighted to match how human ears hear):227

      Concert days per year Venue type Target at nearby homes
      1-3 Urban stadiums and arenas 75 dB(A)
      1-3 Other urban and rural venues 65 dB(A)
      4-12 All Background level plus 15 dB(A)

      The code applies to events between 9am and 11pm.227 Councils write these figures into licensing conditions. In the US there is no national code; limits come from city permits and local noise ordinances.

      Most disputes are about bass. Low frequencies travel farther and pass through walls more easily than the rest of the mix, so a set that measures within limits can still rattle a kitchen a mile away. Curfews, decibel limits and the "agent of change" principle (who adapts when homes and venues sit side by side) are covered in depth in the sister guide at https://noise.cold.fyi/nightlife/.

      The code has not been replaced. A 2020 Institute of Acoustics paper by Wheeler, Sharp and Taherzadeh says it "remains the most up-to-date guidance on the control of music noise at events" in the UK, and that refinements suggested in a 2006 study were never taken forward.228 The paper does not point to any separate national low-frequency standard.

      Who decides whether a festival can happen?

      Local government, mostly, and it can say no.

      Woodstock 50 (2019). The planned anniversary festival needed a permit from the Town of Vernon, New York. The town denied it as "incomplete and filed too late," citing no concrete traffic plan and no access control, and the planning board unanimously rejected the appeal on July 16, 2019. The Oneida County Executive cited "inadequate planning and a dangerously truncated timeframe," and memories of Woodstock 99 in nearby Rome were part of the local reaction.229

      Brockwell Park, London (2025). UK permitted development rights allow temporary use of land, such as a festival, for up to 28 days a year without full planning permission. In May 2025, residents' group Protect Brockwell Park won a High Court ruling quashing Lambeth Council's certificate for that year's events, which included Wide Awake, Field Day, Cross The Tracks, City Splash, Mighty Hoopla and the Lambeth Country Show. The residents said festivals affected the park for about 37 days.

      Organizers reapplied for 24 days and said nothing would be canceled.230 The residents framed it as the loss of a public park; Lambeth framed it as a procedural issue within a sound legal framework.230

      Electric Zoo, New York (2023). On Randall's Island, Friday of the September 1-3 festival was canceled hours before doors. Per Billboard, as reported by EDM Identity, organizers lacked NYC Parks permits for Friday and inspectors found safety problems with the main stage; Saturday opened two hours late.231 On Sunday, gates closed around 6:30 PM, the NYPD estimated about 7,000 tickets had been sold beyond the 42,500 capacity, and a crowd rushed the gates. Mayor Eric Adams said the city would "deal with" the organizers.232 Owner Avant Gardner had bought the festival from LiveStyle in 2022.231

      How do you volunteer at a festival for free?

      The standard deal at UK festivals: work set shifts, put down a refundable deposit, and get free entry and a crew campsite. Volunteers staff gates, stewarding, recycling and bars, and many sign up through charities that fundraise from the work. Stewarding is the name for the crowd and gate side of it; the UK licensing line between stewards and security is in Crowds, heat and keeping people safe.

      Oxfam's 2025 program sought 9,000 volunteers across Glastonbury, Boomtown, Isle of Wight, Boardmasters, Latitude, and Reading and Leeds. The terms: three eight-hour shifts per festival, one refundable deposit, free entry, a secure campsite, crew showers and a meal voucher per shift. Volunteers who do two or more festivals get priority the next year. In 2024, Oxfam volunteers worked over 213,000 hours and helped raise £1.8 million.233

      At Glastonbury 2023, more than 10,000 volunteers gave about 240,000 hours to over 200 nonprofits. Its 3,511 stewards raised £700,000; Oxfam fielded 2,500 volunteers and WaterAid 700.221 The festival also reported over £3.7 million in charitable payments that year.221

      Roskilde takes the model furthest. It is a nonprofit run by The Roskilde Festival Charity Society, gives its surplus to humanitarian and cultural causes (457 million DKK donated since 1972), and runs on about 27,000 volunteers for more than 130,000 participants (official, 2026).234

      US camping festivals run the same trade. Bonnaroo's C'roo volunteer program, as described on its support site (as of November 2026 the page redirects, so check current terms), asks for three six-hour shifts and an on-site training session; volunteers get a wristband for when they are off shift, a car camping pass for the volunteer campgrounds, a T-shirt and a meal token after each shift.235 The page does not mention a deposit.

      12The global festival season

      The short version: There is no off-season for the planet, only a relay. The Southern Hemisphere and the tropics take January to March, the US opens its desert and city-park season in spring, Europe runs flat out from June to August with July the most crowded month, and Rio and the Gulf pick up September and December. The biggest names on each continent run on very different models - a non-profit in Denmark, a dairy farm in England, a two-weekend electronic city in Belgium, a ski resort in Japan - and 2024 to 2026 showed how easily the relay drops a baton: a stage fire, a planned year off, a festival leaving its country, and a flagship canceled twice.

      When is festival season, really?

      It depends which half of the globe you stand on, and how much rain you can tolerate. Southern Hemisphere summer is December to February, so Australia's touring festivals and South America's Lollapalooza editions run while Europe is in coats. In March 2025, Lollapalooza Argentina ran March 21-23 at the Hipódromo de San Isidro with more than 100 artists.239 Laneway's 2026 run covered six cities in eleven days, Auckland to Perth, February 5-15.240

      The US desert and city-park season opens in spring - Coachella in April, which has its own guide, Polo - and EDC Las Vegas in mid-May.241 Europe takes over in June. Then the calendar thins out but never stops: Rock in Rio's 2024 edition ran across two weekends in September,242 and Saudi Arabia's MDLBEAST Soundstorm sits in December, when the desert outside Riyadh is cool enough to dance in.243

      Why is there no Glastonbury in 2026?

      Because the farm needs a rest. Glastonbury takes a fallow year - a planned year off - "roughly every five years," according to AP's 2025 count, and 2026 is one of them.2 The fan-run Glastopedia archive dates the first fallow year to 1988, to "give the land, locals, and organisers a break," with more in 1991, 1996, 2001, 2006, 2012 and 2018; 2020 and 2021 were pandemic cancellations it calls an "enforced fallow year."15 The next festival is set for June 23-27, 2027.17 What the year off does for the farm itself is in Waste, carbon and the town next door.

      The scale it rests from is considerable. In 2025 the license allowed up to 210,000 people on site, "including fans, staff and performers," and the festival sold "a few thousand" fewer tickets than that. General admission sold out in 35 minutes. There were 120 stages, 3,972 performers and more than 4,000 toilets, and single-use plastic bottles have been banned since 2019.2 Sources disagree on the size of the land: AP says 1,000 acres (about 405 hectares),2 Radio X says a 900-acre dairy farm (about 364 hectares).17

      It started small. The first event, in September 1970, the day after Jimi Hendrix died, cost £1 with free milk from the farm and drew 1,500 people (official).13 Reading, the UK's other institution, traces back further, to the National Jazz Festival at Richmond in August 1961; it settled in Reading in 1971 and added a Leeds site in 1999.16

      What happened to the Tomorrowland main stage in 2025?

      It burned down two days before the gates opened, and the festival went ahead anyway. Around 2 PM on Wednesday, July 16, 2025, a fire destroyed the main stage at the De Schorre site in Boom, about 16 km (10 miles) south of Antwerp. No one was hurt. Antwerp prosecutors opened an investigation and said the fire "appeared accidental."265 About 1,000 festival workers were on site; Tomorrowland said damage was "limited to only the main stage," and the DreamVille campsite opened at 10 AM on July 17 as planned.266 A smaller replacement stage was built by July 18, according to Wikipedia's account.26

      The edition was a big one to protect. Belgian presale buyers took 200,000 tickets in 18 minutes, beating the previous year's 27, across two weekends with 400,000 ticket holders in total.97 The two-weekend model is how Tomorrowland has doubled its crowd without doubling its site since 2017 (it was first tried in 2014, for the tenth anniversary); it began in August 2005 with about 10,000 people.26 Belga reported 481 offences and 67 arrests across the two 2025 weekends, and the death of a 35-year-old Canadian woman during the first weekend, with drug use the suspected cause.255

      Why did Exit leave Serbia?

      Exit was founded in 2000 by student activists in Novi Sad, and its 25th edition, July 10-13, 2025 at Petrovaradin Fortress, was announced as its last in Serbia. Organizers cited "financial and political pressure" after the festival backed student-led protests, and said they had been cut off from public funding at all levels. Founder Dušan Kovačević called it "the hardest decision in our 25-year history but we believe that freedom has no price."248 That is the organizers' account; the Serbian government's response was not part of the reporting we used.

      Exit took its brand on a 2026 "world tour"; Mixmag reported in May 2026 that its new summer home would be Long Beach in Ulcinj, Montenegro, with the backing of Montenegro's Prime Minister Milojko Spajić.267 On October 29, 2026, N1 reported that Exit plans to return to Petrovaradin Fortress in Novi Sad in July 2027, a year earlier than organizers had planned, which they credited to "thousands of messages from fans."268

      Japan and Asia: how big is Fuji Rock?

      Fuji Rock is older and more domestic than its European peers, and it has the weather story to prove it. The first edition in 1997 was hit by a typhoon on day one; day two was canceled and there were hypothermia cases. After a year in Tokyo it settled at the Naeba ski resort in Niigata in 1999, with the stated aim of being "the cleanest festival in the world."22

      The 2025 edition, its 28th, drew 122,000 over four days, July 24-27: 16,000 on the pre-festival night, then 33,000, 39,000 and 34,000 (organizer Smash).252 Only about 10% of sales went abroad, to buyers in 60 countries led by Taiwan, South Korea and China, and according to Smash, sales were "the strongest they have been since the COVID-19 pandemic."269 The record stands at 140,000 over three days in 2012.22

      Elsewhere in Asia, US electronic brands are moving in. Wikipedia lists EDC editions in Shanghai (2018), the Seoul area (2019) and Phuket, Thailand (January 2025), where two tourist deaths were reported.244

      Summer Sonic, from the promoter Creativeman, launched in 2000 and runs in Tokyo (ZOZO Marine Stadium and Makuhari Messe) and Osaka (Expo '70 Commemorative Park). For its 25th anniversary in 2026 it planned its largest edition yet, over three days, with a target of a record 330,000 attendees (organizer target, announced December 2025).260 December has two other regional fixtures: Wonderfruit at Siam Country Club near Pattaya, Thailand, which held its tenth edition on December 11-15, 2025,270 and Djakarta Warehouse Project, which ran December 12-14, 2025 at GWK Cultural Park in Bali, Indonesia.271

      Oceania: why was Splendour in the Grass canceled?

      Australia's flagship festival skipped 2024 and then 2025. Splendour in the Grass, running since 2001 and operated by the Live Nation-backed Secret Sounds, said it needed "a little more time to recharge"; sources told Guardian Australia it could not secure overseas headliners of sufficient caliber. The 2024 cancellation came after a lineup with Kylie Minogue, Future and Arcade Fire had been announced.272 In 2025 its North Byron Parklands site, 229 hectares (about 566 acres), was reported for sale at about A$35 million; Live Nation co-owns the festival but not the land.273 Sources disagree on how long it has been there: one says since 2013,272 another "for over twenty years."273

      The wider sector called it a crisis. Australian Festival Association managing director Olly Arkins said "everything that goes into a festival saw significant increases" while ticket prices could not keep pace, and the AFA said artists increasingly prefer arena and stadium tours. Groovin the Moo, Harvest Rock and Spilt Milk were among the festivals axed in 2024, and Pitch Music and Arts was canceled for extreme fire danger.274

      Laneway shows the other Australian model, the touring circuit: one lineup moving city to city. It "began as a little street party in a Melbourne laneway," and its 2026 run marked 21 years; the Queensland leg left its Brisbane site because that site is being prepared for the 2032 Olympics.240

      Latin America: Rock in Rio and the Lollapalooza circuit

      Rock in Rio is the region's legacy giant. Its 2024 edition was billed as the festival's 40th anniversary275 and ran seven days, September 13-15 and 19-22. A finance outlet summarizing organizer figures puts the cumulative audience at "over 11.2 million" across 130 days since 1985.242

      Lollapalooza, which began as a US touring package on July 18, 1991 in Arizona,19 now has a March circuit in Chile, Argentina and Brazil. Argentina's 10th edition in 2025 let children under 10 in free with an adult.239

      The Rio Times reported that the 2024 edition drew 730,000 people across its seven days (reported, March 2025; the article does not name the source of the figure).276 Lollapalooza Chile was first held on April 2-3, 2011 at Parque O'Higgins in Santiago, and Wikipedia gives its attendance record as 225,000 in 2022, at Parque Bicentenario in Cerrillos. Lollapalooza Brasil followed in São Paulo in 2012.277

      Africa: Afro Nation and Nyege Nyege

      The continent's two best-known festival names came from opposite directions. Afro Nation is a diaspora-facing beach festival that started on August 1, 2019 at Praia da Rocha in Portimão, Portugal; Wikipedia gives its 2023 Portugal crowd as about 40,000 from 140 countries and says 90% of festival-goers travel from abroad (without dating that share), with editions also in Ghana (2022), Puerto Rico, Miami and Detroit, and Live Nation among its organizers.278 Nyege Nyege is an underground electronic festival founded in 2015 in Jinja, Uganda, "the city at the source of the Nile." It drew about 20,000 people in 2023, and its ninth edition in 2024 moved to a 12-acre (about 5-hectare) site across from the old one.279

      Middle East: how big is MDLBEAST Soundstorm?

      Saudi Arabia's Soundstorm, at Banban outside Riyadh, reported "over 180,000 people" on the first day of its 2021 edition, with more than 500,000 expected over four days.243 Those are reported first-day and expected figures, not an audited total. Coverage of the Saudi state's role in entertainment is contested; this guide reports the attendance and leaves the argument to the sources.

      The US: EDC, Lollapalooza and the weather

      US megafestivals cluster from spring to late summer. EDC Las Vegas at Las Vegas Motor Speedway is the attendance giant: "over 525,000 festival-goers" across three nights in 2024, with next-year "Owl Passes" going on sale for $5 deposits.241 Its founding date depends on whom you ask: Wikipedia traces an Electric Daisy Carnival to Chino, California in 1991,244 while Insomniac's own history dates the first EDC to 1997 at the Shrine Expo Hall in Los Angeles, with the move to Las Vegas in 2011.27

      Lollapalooza Chicago is the city-park model at scale: "roughly 115,000 attendees each day" in Grant Park in 2025, with 12 arrests and 42 hospitalizations, the lowest in at least six years, per city figures.253 It had more than 170 artists, with Sabrina Carpenter closing Sunday.280 The festival stopped touring in 1997 and became a yearly Chicago event in 2005.20

      Why was Bonnaroo 2025 canceled?

      Rain. On Friday, June 13, 2025, organizers evacuated the fields at The Farm in Manchester, Tennessee, and canceled the rest of the four-day festival after storms, flooding and a National Weather Service forecast of more heavy rain: "We are beyond gutted, but we must make the safest decision and cancel the remainder of Bonnaroo." Four-day pass holders were initially offered 75% back; one-day passes were fully refunded.247 Bonnaroo began on June 21-23, 2002, when more than 70,000 tickets sold out in advance,24 and it runs on a 700-acre (about 283-hectare) farm; Wikipedia notes a 2021 cancellation for Hurricane Ida rain as well.25

      On June 20, 2025, organizers raised that to a full refund - "we are updating the refund to 100% rather than the 75% originally offered" - covering four-day admission and four-day camping bought through Front Gate Tickets, paid to the original payment method "in as little as 30 days."281

      Is Burning Man a festival?

      Not by its own account. In the official Burning Man Journal in 2012, Caveat Magister wrote that "Burning Man refuses to acknowledge the category," calling it "an engine of possibility" and citing a Yahoo piece that conceded it is "more like a makeshift city than a festival."282 A 2017 Journal piece called it "a social experiment for the ages" and Black Rock City a place "born each August out of a shared intention to create," and argued that the media's use of "festival" misleads newcomers.256 It often appears on festival lists anyway, which is why it is here: it inverts the model, with no headliners and no spectators by design.

      Elsewhere in the world: the 2024-2026 squeeze

      The season's gaps are not only weather and fire. In the UK, the Association of Independent Festivals, a trade body for independents, counted a record 78 festivals canceled, postponed or closed in 2024 and 249 lost since 2019 as of June 2025, and proposed a tax relief for events under 30,000 capacity.3 It had earlier asked for VAT on tickets to drop from 20% to 5%.38 Those are a trade body's figures; the UK government's response was not in our sources. In the US, Pitchfork said in November 2024 that it would not hold its Chicago festival in 2025, after 19 years, and gave no reason; fans blamed Condé Nast cost-cutting, others "a struggling market for music festivals in general."44

      In New England

      New England is where the multi-day outdoor festival template is usually said to start, though the claim has a rival: Los Angeles ran the annual Cavalcade of Jazz at Wrigley Field from 1945.4 The Newport Jazz Festival opened on July 17, 1954 with tickets at "$3 to $5" and Billie Holiday and Dizzy Gillespie on the bill.6 The Newport Folk Festival followed in 1959.7 Both now run at Fort Adams State Park under the non-profit Newport Festivals Foundation, in place since 2011.4

      Boston Calling debuted on Memorial Day weekend in 2013 and has been a late-May festival since 2016;9 since 2017 it has used Harvard's athletic complex in Allston.283 There is no 2026 edition; organizers announced a return for June 4-6, 2027,57 but in October 2026 Axios Boston reported the festival's future "in doubt" with organizers silent.138 The rest of the regional calendar, from Green River to the July 4 Pops concert, is in New England: from Newport to Boston Calling.

      13New England: from Newport to Boston Calling

      The short version: New England has a small festival map with a long history. Newport Jazz (1954) and Newport Folk (1959) went through almost every stage a festival can go through - rich patrons, riots, exile, corporate title sponsors - and both now run under a nonprofit foundation at a state park, where Folk sells out before naming a single act. Boston Calling (2013) went from City Hall Plaza to Harvard's fields in Allston, from an indie startup to a Live Nation subsidiary, and skipped 2026; organizers announced a return for June 4-6, 2027, but by October 2026 Axios Boston was calling its future "in doubt." Around them sit mid-size festivals in Greenfield, North Adams and Marshfield, a free July 4 Pops concert bigger than any of them, and a tiered Boston permit system.

      How did a jazz weekend in Newport become the template?

      The first Newport Jazz Festival in 1954 was billed as the "First Annual American Jazz Festival" and held at the Newport Casino. Newport socialites Elaine and Louis Lorillard founded it and hired George Wein to organize it.4 Sources disagree on the opening crowd: Wikipedia says about 13,000 over two days, while the Rhode Island Historical Society's RI Rocks project says about 11,000.4284

      In 1955 the Lorillards bought the Belcourt estate to host the festival, neighbors blocked the plan over disturbance, and the concerts moved to Freebody Park.284

      Schlitz and KOOL were title sponsors - companies whose name goes on the festival itself - in the 1970s; it was the JVC Jazz Festival from 1984 to 2008, and CareFusion replaced JVC in 2009.4 In 2011 Wein set up the nonprofit Newport Festivals Foundation, which has run both the jazz and folk festivals since.4

      The 72nd festival ran July 31 to August 2, 2026, at Fort Adams after three sellouts in a row. The foundation is a 501(c)(3) focused on music education and artist relief, and Nate Smith took over as artistic director from Christian McBride, who had held the job for about a decade.58 The foundation says its Artist Gives program has made more than 197 grants to music education programs in over 35 states since 2018 (foundation figure, reported 2026).58

      What happened at the 1960 Newport Jazz riot?

      On the Saturday of the 1960 festival, thousands of people who could not get into sold-out shows roamed the streets and clashed with police. Wikipedia reports about 200 arrests, a town record, says the National Guard was called, and says the last two days were canceled.4 RI Rocks also says the National Guard was called, and puts the end of the festival after Muddy Waters's Sunday set.284 The two accounts differ on exactly when it stopped; the arrest count has only the one source here.

      The crowds kept growing: Wikipedia reports excess crowds estimated at 50,000 in 1969.4

      Why did Newport Jazz move to New York, and how did it come back?

      In 1971, with the Allman Brothers on the bill, more fans arrived than Festival Field could hold. More than 12,000 people on a nearby hillside broke through the fence during Dionne Warwick's set, the stage was rushed, equipment was destroyed and the festival was halted.4284

      Wein moved the event to New York in 1972 as "Newport Jazz Festival-New York," using venues including Yankee Stadium and Radio City Music Hall.284

      It came back to Newport in 1981, at Fort Adams State Park, as a daytime-only, alcohol-free event on three stages.284 A fenced state park on the harbor was a different proposition from open fields in town.

      When did Newport Folk start, and what really happened when Dylan went electric?

      Newport Folk was first held on July 11, 1959, at Freebody Park. Its founders were George Wein, Albert Grossman, Pete Seeger, Theodore Bikel and Oscar Brand.7

      On July 25, 1965, Bob Dylan played with members of the Paul Butterfield Blues Band, his first plugged-in professional set. Some fans booed.7 Beyond that, the story is less settled than the legend. Wikipedia says the hostility came from a small group of folk purists and that the crowd was generally enthusiastic, and also says accounts of who booed and why are still contested.7

      Britannica sets the witness accounts side by side. Dylan played three electric songs - "Maggie's Farm," "Like a Rolling Stone" and an early "It Takes a Lot to Laugh, It Takes a Train to Cry" - then came back alone with an acoustic guitar. Robert Shelton reported that the audience "registered hostility" from the first song; Anthony Scaduto described scattered boos and applause and mostly bewildered silence; Eric von Schmidt blamed a poor sound mix, with people near the stage shouting that they could not hear; and Al Kooper, who was on stage, put the booing down mainly to the short set.285

      The folk festival ran 1959-1960 and 1963-1969. It was not held in 1970 because of money problems and local controversy tied to the jazz festival. Wein's longtime producer Bob Jones persuaded the Weins to revive it in 1985.7

      It has been at Fort Adams since that revival. It became for-profit in the mid-1980s, and in 2011 announced a return to nonprofit status under the Newport Festivals Foundation.7 Wein hired Jay Sweet in 2008, and Wikipedia credits the festival's later direction to Sweet.7

      Why does Newport Folk sell out before the lineup is announced?

      Newport Folk's 2026 tickets went on sale on Wednesday, February 4, 2026, before any artist was named. They "appeared to sell out within seconds" on DICE, and buyers were sent to a waitlist. Organizers warned against "speculative" resale listings and noted there are no PDF tickets.286

      Selling without a lineup is a blind sale. Newport built up to it. Saturday sold out in 2011, both days in 2012, Saturday and Sunday in 2013 after the festival grew to three days, and all three days months ahead in 2014. Wikipedia says it has sold out every year since, as of 2023, and that it now announces its lineup bit by bit after the tickets are gone.7

      Part of what buyers are paying for is the surprise guest, an act that was never announced. Wikipedia lists James Taylor and My Morning Jacket (2015), Kris Kristofferson (2016), Roger Waters (2017), Mumford & Sons (2018), Dolly Parton (2019), and Paul Simon and Joni Mitchell (2022).7

      The small size helps. The 2026 festival ran July 24-26 at Fort Adams with more than 10,000 people expected on the grounds each day.287 Rhode Island's governor's office described Fort Adams capacity crowds as 10,000 in 2023.8 A fixed, small capacity plus a loyal audience is the condition for selling out in seconds. Boats gather on the harbor to hear the sets for free.287

      COVID canceled the 2020 festival, and 2021 was restructured as two three-day events in July.7 On July 25, 2025, patrons were told to shelter in place as severe weather hit on opening day.288

      For 2026, Relix reported single-day, two-day and three-day passes at $118.45, $226.60 and $314.15, "with fees factored in" (official prices, as reported).289

      Who owns the Newport festivals, and what do they bring Rhode Island?

      Nobody owns them in the shareholder sense: both are produced by the Newport Festivals Foundation, a 501(c)(3).58 Fort Adams is Rhode Island state land, so the state has a stake. A 2012 study commissioned by the foundation at the Rhode Island Department of Environmental Management's request put direct spending in the state from Jazz and Folk at about $5.09 million; earlier University of Rhode Island studies estimated $2.11 million (1994 Jazz) and $1.60 million (1997 Folk).238 In 2023 the governor's office said nearly 90% of attendees come to Newport for the festivals, and cited a URI estimate of $64 million in economic output for Fort Adams in 2016 - a figure for the whole park, not the festivals alone.8

      The festivals hold their place at Fort Adams under a state license. On July 17, 2018, the Rhode Island Department of Environmental Management (DEM) and the Newport Festivals Foundation signed a 25-year license for the festivals at the park, alongside a 40-year lease on Building 16, the former Museum of Yachting, which the foundation agreed to restore with private funds.290 During festival weeks DEM temporarily restricts parts of the park; in 2022 that covered shore fishing, scuba diving and the state piers and docks, while the boat ramp stayed open.291

      How did Boston Calling start, and why did it leave City Hall Plaza?

      Boston Calling debuted on Memorial Day weekend, May 2013, at City Hall Plaza. It was created by Brian Appel and Mike Snow, who had worked at WFNX, an indie alternative station that stopped broadcasting in 2012 after Clear Channel bought it.9 It ran twice a year, in May and September, through 2015, then once in 2016; early editions drew about 20,000-22,000 fans.9

      On May 27, 2016, WBUR reported the move to Harvard's athletic complex at 65 North Harvard St. in Allston, under a rental agreement starting May 2017. Appel said the move would let the festival "expand and enrich Boston Calling while still keeping it proudly located in Boston," and Mayor Marty Walsh backed it. The 2016 edition was the sixth and last at City Hall Plaza.283 Wikipedia puts 2017 attendance at about 40,000.9

      The first Allston edition came days after the Manchester Arena attack, and security was stepped up; the details are in Crowds, heat and keeping people safe.159

      By 2023, Live Nation Entertainment, through its subsidiary Boston Calling Events. The chain of owners follows the wider consolidation described in the section on who owns festivals:

      A controlling interest is enough ownership to direct the company. At the 2016 sale, Aaron Dessner of The National was curating, and the plan was one three-day Memorial Day edition a year in Allston with "expanded capacity."292

      Ticket prices give a sense of scale: in 2019, three-day passes ran from $249 (early) to over $1,100 (VIP), and single-day general admission was $114 (reported).237 In 2022 the festival added a stage for local artists, the Tivoli Audio Orange Stage, which had 12 local performances in 2023.293

      Can you hear Boston Calling from Cambridge?

      In 2017, yes, a lot of people could. The Allston site sits across the Charles from Cambridge homes. Sound leaking across the river drew many complaints; Harvard's government-relations director Tom Lucey called it "horrific" for some residents and said the sound engineer was fired. The festival's Boston permit limit was 70 decibels.236 Noise traveling past the site is sound bleed.

      For 2018 the festival hired a new team, Clearwing Productions, used L-Acoustics engineers and Soundvision "sound maps" for each stage, and paid an independent consultant to monitor neighborhoods. Cambridge Police logged 17 noise complaints (15 from Cambridge, 2 from Brookline), down nearly half.236

      In 2019 one band on Saturday overrode the agreed sound settings. The promoter put an engineer over every act on Sunday, and a Cambridge police spokesperson said Sunday complaints fell by more than half.237 The same year the Harvard Square Business Association's director said the festival brought about 25,000 extra people through the square over the weekend (method not stated), and a neighborhood association leader asked for a direct financial contribution to the area; no agreement was described.237

      The odd part is jurisdiction. The noise was permitted in Boston but the complaints went to Cambridge Police, so a festival can be inside its permit in one city and still cause trouble in the next. More on curfews and noise rules is at https://noise.cold.fyi/nightlife/.

      What happened at Boston Calling 2024?

      Boston EMS reported about 800 medical encounters and 23 people sent to hospitals over the 2024 weekend; Sunday alone had 412 people assisted and 13 hospital transports. Attendees described crushing, water lines of 20-60 minutes, and disabled patrons pushed out of accessible viewing areas.294 The worst of it came on Sunday, May 26, during Hozier's set; Mayor Michelle Wu said EMS transfers far exceeded other days.171

      Is Boston Calling coming back?

      Not in 2026. A return for June 4-6, 2027 has been announced, but as of October 2026 organizers had said nothing more publicly. On June 20, 2025, organizers announced there would be no 2026 festival and gave no reason; C3 Presents called it a "short break." The announced 2027 dates are the first weekend in June, not Memorial Day. It is not clear whether it will return to Harvard's fields; Harvard did not comment.57295 Axios Boston ran the headline "Boston Calling's future in doubt as organizers remain silent."138

      As for why: the only stated reason came secondhand from Mayor Michelle Wu, who told WBZ NewsRadio that organizers pointed to how busy 2026 would be, "between the 250th anniversary celebrations, between FIFA coming," and the pressure that would put on hotels, venues and sponsors.2969 Organizers themselves gave no reason. A former bar manager, Drew Fosdick, speculated to the Globe about friction after Live Nation arrived; that is his view, not an official explanation.57

      Is the Boston Pops July 4 concert free?

      Yes. The Boston Symphony Orchestra describes the Boston Pops Fireworks Spectacular as "Boston's free July 4th fireworks celebration on the Charles River Esplanade"; 2026 materials mention a drone show and list Mass Cultural Council as the website sponsor.305

      Arthur Fiedler's July 4 concerts date to 1929, before the Hatch Shell existed. In 1974 about 75,000 people came to hear the Pops play Tchaikovsky's 1812 Overture with cannons, fireworks and church bells; that first event cost about $10,000, paid by David Mugar and his family.306 The 1976 Bicentennial drew about 400,000. By 1998 the budget was near $1 million, mostly funded by Mugar, who had never sought or accepted corporate sponsorship.306

      A free event has no ticket count, so every crowd figure is an estimate.

      No official count turned up for this guide. NBC Boston described the 2025 show, the 51st, as drawing "hundreds of thousands of people" to the Esplanade, without naming a source for the figure (reported, July 2025).307

      Weather is managed like a festival's, at city scale. In 2014 Tropical Storm (later Hurricane) Arthur moved the show from July 4 to July 3, with Storrow Drive closed from noon.308 The fireworks started early, at 10 p.m., and State Police cleared the oval right after because of a severe thunderstorm warning; no injuries were reported.309

      In Boston: what permits does an outdoor event need?

      The Mayor's Office of Consumer Affairs and Licensing (MOCAL) issues the final Outdoor Public Event permit. As of the city's 2026 page, events sort into three tiers:310

      Tier What puts you there Minimum notice
      Tier 1 One day or less, stationary, 2 or fewer blocks, 149 or fewer people 10 business days
      Tier 2 Moving, or more than 2 blocks, or 150-250 people 20 business days
      Tier 3 More than one day, recurring, a street with MBTA stops, a carnival, a bounce house, alcohol, or more than 250 people 30 business days

      The steps run: the property owner's written approval, a site plan (with a 20-foot emergency lane on public streets), an online application, a committee meeting if required, and the signed application - including the Police Captain's signature - at least one week out.310 Fire Department tent (400 square feet and up), cooking and generator permits (no gasoline generators), temporary food and street occupancy permits stack on top. The one-time entertainment license is usually issued last. Tiers 2 and 3 need insurance, and ads must say "Pending the license approvals by the City of Boston" until everything is approved.310

      Any event with a capacity of 1,500 or more must also file a Special Events Emergency Action Plan and site map with the Office of Emergency Management. The city's wording: "Without this your permit application will not be approved."311

      Newport is different: the festivals there sit on Rhode Island state parkland, so Boston's rules don't apply.

          19Quick answers

          How do festival RFID wristbands work?

          The chip in the band carries a unique ID number, not your personal details, and readers look it up in the festival's database at gates and bars. NFC chips read at about 4 cm for payments, and UHF chips read at about 1-6 m for fast gate entry, according to a 2026 vendor guide.

          How do I get a refund of my cashless festival balance?

          Request it online after the festival, inside the published window if there is one. Fees in 2025-2026 examples ran from 0.50 euros at splash! to 3.50 euros at Dour, and Dour says credit not claimed by its deadline is lost.

          How many toilets do you need per person at a festival?

          UK council guidance for events open six hours or more starts at 1 toilet per 100 women and 1 toilet per 500 men plus 1 urinal per 150 men. Shorter events start at 1 per 150 women and 1 per 600 men plus 1 urinal per 175.

          When is festival season in Europe?

          Europe's peak runs from June to August, with July the most crowded month. In 2025 Exit, Mad Cool, FIB and Tomorrowland all ran in July, and Awakenings ran in July 2026.

          What is an early bird festival ticket?

          It is the cheapest tier, sold first in limited numbers, and the price steps up once it sells out. Lollapalooza's 2013 early-bird three-day passes were $200; the next tier was $235.

          Does Glastonbury make a profit?

          Yes. Glastonbury reported revenue of £68.4 million and profit of £5.9 million for 2024, per Companies House filings reported by DJ Mag, after record losses in 2020 and 2021.

          How much does it cost to go to a music festival?

          One financial planner estimated $800 to $3,000 for a US festival trip in 2024, including ticket, travel, lodging and food. A 2024 Opinium poll found 65% of UK adults think festivals are becoming too expensive.

          Is Boston Calling coming back in 2027?

          Possibly, but it is not confirmed. There was no Boston Calling in 2026, and in June 2025 organizers announced a return for June 4-6, 2027, but as of October 2026 they had not confirmed that edition and Axios Boston reported the festival's future "in doubt" with organizers silent.
